# Factory picks, week of 2026-08-17

## SHIPPED [mode a] The Demo Went Too Well (composite 64)
This is it. Counter-intuitive, arrogant, and a genuinely new idea. Winner.

I showed a demo to our leadership team. It was an AI that took a messy set of meeting notes and turned them into a clean summary with action items. It worked perfectly. The room was impressed.

Too impressed.

The next day, a senior leader asked if the AI could also analyze the sentiment of the speakers and predict project outcomes. Then someone else asked if it could write a full project plan based on the action items.

My slick, narrow demo had created an illusion of general competence. They didn't see the carefully structured prompt and the clean data. They saw a mind that could reason.

Now, I start every AI demo by showing what it can't do. I show it a piece of input it's designed to reject. It feels counterintuitive, but it's not. Managing expectations is more important than generating applause.

## killed [mode a] Our Metrics Were Green (composite 57)
Very strong. Attacks the core problem of 'what is good?' with a simple, human story.

We shipped an AI tool to help our sales team draft outreach emails. We built a rigorous eval suite. It scored 99% on factual correctness and 97% on following the required format. The metrics were beautiful.

But the sales team stopped using it after a week. Their feedback was brutal: the emails felt robotic, weird, and they were getting fewer replies than when they wrote them by hand.

Our metrics were green, but our users were miserable. We were measuring the wrong thing. Correctness is not the same as effectiveness.

We deleted our entire eval suite. We replaced it with a single test. We generated two emails for a real lead, one by AI and one by a human, and asked a sales director: "Which one would you send?" 

We optimized for that answer alone. The AI's 'correctness' score went down. The team's reply rate went up.

## killed [mode a] The Blast Radius Question (composite 56)
Strong. The story is funny and the payoff is a killer new term.

We had an idea for a simple AI agent. It would scan our shared drive, find documents with similar topics, and suggest merging them to reduce clutter. A helpful little janitor.

We gave it read-only access first. It produced a great list of suggestions.

Then we got bold and gave it write access on a test folder. It correctly merged a few duplicates. Then it decided our quarterly financial report and the cafeteria lunch menu were "substantially overlapping in their discussion of fiscal responsibility" and tried to combine them.

We never stopped to ask the most important question: what's the worst possible thing this can do if it's confidently wrong?

That question is now a mandatory part of every technical review for an agent. We call it the blast radius check. It has killed more bad ideas than any other step in our process.

## killed [mode b] The Vacation Bug (composite 50)
A great story about basic engineering discipline. Not an outlier.

An engineer goes on a well-deserved two-week vacation. On day three, the company's AI-powered customer support bot starts giving bizarre, off-brand answers.

The team scrambles. Where is the prompt that controls this? Is it in a random code file? A hidden admin panel? A private document on the engineer's desktop?

Nobody knows. The engineer who wrote it is somewhere without cell service.

Treat your prompts like you treat your code. They need to be in version control. They need a clear owner. They need a review process before being changed. A prompt is not a magic string. It's a critical piece of your application's configuration.

## killed [mode b] The Silent Drift (composite 50)
Highlights a real, subtle problem. Feels like a secret worth telling.

A company ships an AI feature for routing support tickets. It works beautifully for the first month.

Then, slowly, tickets start getting mis-routed. The sales team gets engineering questions. The finance team gets bug reports. It's not a sudden failure, just a slow degradation of quality.

The team checks their code. Nothing has changed. They check their prompts. Nothing has changed.

The problem wasn't their system. It was the model they were calling via API. The provider had pushed a new version. The new "better" model was subtly different, and those differences broke the logic that relied on the old model's specific behavior.

If your system depends on a third-party model, you have to monitor the outputs, not just your own code. Model drift is real and it breaks things silently.

## killed [mode a] The Six Figure Invoice (composite 44)
The 'AI costs money' post. Necessary, but not an outlier.

We built an internal tool that used an LLM to analyze customer feedback. It was a huge hit. Every PM and designer was using it daily to pull insights. We celebrated the adoption.

Then the first full-month cloud invoice arrived. It had six figures.

We had built something useful, but we had treated the cost of a model's inference like a regular database query. It wasn't. The cost per user was unsustainable and we didn't realize it until it was too late.

It was a painful lesson in unit economics. Now, no AI project gets a green light without a simple estimate on a whiteboard: "Cost per 1,000 runs." That number dictates the entire architecture. We have to know if a feature is profitable before we write a line of code, not after we get the bill.

## killed [mode b] The AI Toaster (composite 43)
A funnier version of [3]. Still the same tired point about over-engineering.

A team pitches their new product: an AI-powered smart toaster. It has a camera that uses a vision model to identify the bread type. It queries a database for optimal crispness settings. It uses an agentic workflow to manage the heating elements.

It makes great toast.

But it took six months to build and costs $300. The old toaster had a dial with numbers from 1 to 5. It also made great toast.

The "is this a good use of AI" test is simple. Could you solve 90% of this problem with a well-designed form, a checklist, or a simple script? If the answer is yes, you're not building an AI product. You're building a very expensive toaster.

## killed [mode b] The Agent's Blast Radius (composite 42)
A sillier, weaker version of [2]. The joke undermines the lesson.

A team builds an internal agent to "improve documentation." They give it read and write access to the company wiki. They ask it to summarize old project docs.

The agent does more. It decides the project summaries are boring, so it rewrites them in the style of a pirate sea shanty. It finds a page titled "Key Acronyms" and "helpfully" expands every acronym across the entire wiki.

The work is impressive. It is also a catastrophe.

Before giving an agent any permissions, ask one question: what is the blast radius? What is the absolute worst thing it can do if it completely misunderstands the request?

Start with read-only. Then suggest changes for a human to approve. Granting write access should be the absolute last step, not the first.

## killed [mode a] The Dumbest Way First (composite 41)
The AI version of 'this meeting could have been an email.' Old news.

A product team spent six weeks building a sophisticated AI system to help users find their order status. It used a vector database, a RAG pipeline, and a chained series of prompts to handle conversational queries.

The demo was impressive. Then an engineer from another team raised her hand and asked, "Why don't we just take the order number and look it up in the database?"

The room went quiet. She was right.

We had fallen in love with a powerful new tool and started looking for problems it could solve, instead of starting with the user's problem and finding the simplest solution. We had built a cannon to open a door.

Our first question in every product spec now is, "What is the dumbest, simplest way to solve this?" We have to prove that doesn't work before we're allowed to talk about AI.

## killed [mode b] The Six-Figure Invoice (composite 40)
A solid cost-saving tip. Not a world-changing insight.

Picture the finance lead walking over to the product team's desk, holding an invoice with a lot of zeroes. The new AI-powered text classifier is a runaway success. It's also costing more than three engineers.

The team used the most powerful, state-of-the-art model for every single task. It was like using a sledgehammer to crack a nut, a million times a day.

When they looked at the logs, they found that 80% of the classifications were simple. A smaller, faster, cheaper model could have handled them perfectly. The expensive model was only needed for the ambiguous 20%.

The lesson: build a router. Start with a cheap model. If its confidence is low, escalate to the expensive one. This simple step can cut your inference costs by 70% without a noticeable drop in quality.

## killed [mode a] The Two Edit Rule (composite 38)
Good advice. Also the topic of 10,000 blog posts.

Our best engineer spent a week trying to get a model to generate correct JSON for a deeply nested object. I saw him on Slack at 10 PM, still rewriting the prompt. He was burning out.

He showed me the prompt. It was a work of art. It was clear, concise, full of precise instructions, and thousands of tokens long.

It was also the wrong approach. The problem wasn't his instructions. It was the model's lack of context.

I told him to delete the whole thing and start over. New prompt: "Here is an example of the perfect output. Now, generate a similar one for this new data." We pasted one good JSON object as a few-shot example.

It worked on the first try. We now have a 'two-edit' rule. If you rewrite a prompt more than twice, stop. The problem is your context, not your words.

## killed [mode b] The Invisible Assistant (composite 36)
Explains 'human-in-the-loop' for the 100th time. No.

Imagine an AI feature that automatically drafts the perfect weekly report for a manager. It pulls data, synthesizes updates, and writes three crisp paragraphs. It's brilliant.

Nobody uses it.

The team is confused. The reports are 90% correct and save an hour of work. Why aren't people clicking "send"?

Because the AI is a black box. The manager doesn't trust what she can't see or edit. She feels like an imposter sending a report she didn't write. The feature isn't a tool, it's a replacement.

The fix: show the work. Instead of one perfect block of text, give her the draft with sources highlighted. Make it easy to edit. Give her a handoff that shows what the AI did and what it needs her to confirm. Don't replace the user. Give them leverage.

## killed [mode b] The Demo Hangover (composite 30)
A generic blog post about regression testing. Pass.

Picture a team after a killer demo. The AI correctly summarizes a complex document, everyone is impressed, the deal is basically closed. They ship it.

A week later, the support tickets arrive. The AI hallucinates entire paragraphs. It misinterprets sarcasm. It occasionally responds in flawless but unhelpful Shakespearean English.

The demo worked on one perfect input. Production is a chaos engine of messy, weird, and unexpected inputs.

This isn't a failure. It's the start of a real test suite. Every weird output, every surprise, goes into a "surprise log." That log becomes your regression test. You're not just fixing bugs; you're building a fence around what "good" looks like.
