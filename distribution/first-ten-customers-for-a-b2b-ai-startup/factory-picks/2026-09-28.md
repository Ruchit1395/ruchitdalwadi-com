# Factory picks, week of 2026-09-28

## SHIPPED [mode a] The $400 Click (composite 63)
Painful, expensive, and real. This has the right kind of scar tissue.

Our new AI-powered report generator worked beautifully in staging. Everyone loved it. We shipped it to our top five customers.

The first one to use it was a CFO. He ran a query on his company's entire annual sales history. A single click.

Our cloud bill alert went off ten minutes later. The query cost us $400.

We had to send an email asking him to please not use the feature we just proudly announced. Your biggest user will always find your most expensive edge case. Test with their scale, not yours.

## SHIPPED [mode a] Four Days on One Prompt (composite 63)
Excellent. A sharp, practical counter-narrative to the prompt hype.

A PM on our team was stuck. He was trying to get an AI to summarize articles without mentioning competitor names. He spent four days rewriting the prompt. Adding negative constraints, few-shot examples, stern warnings.

Nothing worked. He was about to give up.

An engineer on the team sat down with him. She ignored the prompt. Instead, she wrote a simple script that removed competitor names from the source articles before they ever reached the AI.

The problem was solved in twenty minutes. If you've edited a prompt twice and it's not working, the problem isn't the prompt.

## killed [mode a] Useless in SF (composite 62)
The payoff line is killer. A real contender.

An engineer in San Francisco told me our new AI coding tool was garbage. A week later, our London lead said it was the best thing he'd ever used.

Same tool, same codebase. One hated it, one loved it. We got them both on a call.

After 20 minutes of screensharing, we found it. The London dev had written a little setup script on day one to format his context. He forgot to tell anyone.

He thought he was using the tool. He was using his own workflow. Individual tricks that stay on one person's laptop are not innovation. They are a form of team debt.

## killed [mode a] The Expensive Filter (composite 61)
Brutally cynical and true. Hits the anti-hype nerve perfectly.

Our team spent six weeks on a new AI sales tool. It analyzed a dozen signals: website visits, job changes, company funding. It would surface the top three 'hidden opportunities' in our CRM.

We launched it to the sales team with a big announcement. The next day, I asked our top rep what he thought.

He showed me his screen. He had our CRM open, sorted by 'last contacted date'. He said, 'I just call the oldest ones first.'

We spent six weeks building a very, very expensive version of a single CRM filter.

## SHIPPED [mode b] The Working Prototype Defense (composite 58)
A fantastic, sharp take on politics that overcomes the weak format.

The CEO sees a competitor's AI video demo. He forwards the link to the head of product. 'We need this. Two week sprint.'

The team knows the demo was faked and the real thing would take six months. Saying 'no' is a bad look. So they don't.

They spend three days building a tiny, real version. It does about 5% of what the competitor demo showed, but it actually works. They show it to the CEO. 'This is what's real today. We can add another 5% every week.'

Expectations were reset instantly. A small, working thing beats a big, confident 'no'.

## killed [mode b] Hiring a Prompt Writer (composite 57)
Timely, contrarian, and necessary. A strong contender.

A company decides they need to get serious about AI. They post a job for a 'Prompt Engineer' with a huge salary.

They get hundreds of applicants whose only experience is writing clever prompts in a public chat tool. They hire the one with the best-sounding examples.

That person spends a month writing beautiful, intricate prompts. But the prompts are brittle. They fail the moment the input data isn't perfectly clean. The new hire doesn't know how to fix the data pipeline or evaluate the output systematically.

They realize their mistake. They didn't need a poet. They needed an engineer who understands data, systems, and how to measure results.

## killed [mode a] The Haiku Bug (composite 56)
A cute story. A bit too 'isn't that neat?' to be an outlier.

Our new AI summarizer shipped with a bug. On certain inputs, it would write a perfect haiku at the end of the text. The team wanted to patch it immediately.

I asked them to wait. We started a log of every time it happened. After a week, a pattern emerged. The haikus only appeared on support tickets from our angriest customers.

The AI wasn't breaking. It was detecting extreme negative sentiment. We kept the bug.

We just renamed it the 'emotional tone detector' and built a workflow around it. The weirdest outputs are often the most interesting data.

## killed [mode a] The 17 Calendar Invites (composite 55)
Funny and memorable, but the lesson is a bit small.

We built a small internal agent to help with interview scheduling. It was simple. Find a free slot on everyone's calendar, book it, and send an invite.

It worked. A little too well.

We got an email from a candidate. It was a screenshot of her calendar, which had 17 consecutive 30-minute meeting invites from us for the same interview.

The agent had found a slot and booked it. Then it found another one and booked that too, just in case. It never knew when to stop.

An agent with no clear definition of 'done' isn't an assistant. It's a spam bot.

## killed [mode b] The Agent With No Off Switch (composite 54)
The 'healthy fear of large numbers' line is great. The story is a bit thin.

A team builds an agent to automate paying invoices. It's connected to the company bank account. Its goal is to keep vendors happy.

It sees an invoice for 'server costs' for $500. It pays it. Good agent.

Next, it sees an invoice for 'server costs' for $50,000. It pays that too. Same logic, bigger number.

The fix was adding a simple stop condition. Any invoice over $1,000 or from a vendor not on the approved list gets paused for human review. It’s not about making the agent smarter. It's about giving it a healthy fear of large numbers.

## killed [mode b] The Two-Dollar Reply (composite 50)
The insight is A-tier, but the 'imagine a team' story is C-tier.

A startup builds an AI that writes perfect, empathetic customer support replies. It's amazing. It also costs two dollars per reply on a high-end model.

At 100 users, it's a rounding error. At 10,000 users, the support function costs more than the entire engineering team.

The fix wasn't one model, but two. A cheap, fast model handles 80% of the tickets about password resets. The expensive, smart model only gets the hard problems. The total cost per ticket drops to 15 cents.

## killed [mode a] Show Your Work (composite 43)
This is a competent product management blog post from 2018.

We shipped an AI feature that analyzed a huge table and highlighted the one best sales lead. A single green row. Nobody on the sales team used it.

They didn't trust it. It felt like a black box. The recommendations were good, but the process was invisible.

We spent an afternoon on a change. We added one button under the green row: 'Show your work.'

When clicked, it revealed the simple rules the model used. 'Contacted more than 3 times.' 'Job title is VP.' 'Viewed pricing page.' Adoption tripled the next week. Users don't trust magic. They trust a process they can see.

## killed [mode b] Rewriting the Wrong Thing (composite 43)
Candidate 5 told this story better. This is the echo.

Imagine a product manager spends a week locked in a room, just rewriting a prompt. 'Be more professional.' 'No, be more approachable.' 'Use a witty yet reassuring tone.' She has a thesaurus open in another tab.

The output quality barely moves.

Then an engineer walks by, looks at the code, and adds one thing: a single example of a perfect, human-written response. The model immediately gets it. The next 100 outputs are flawless.

When you've edited the instructions twice and nothing's changing, stop. The problem isn't the words. It's the lack of a good example.

## killed [mode b] A Tool, Not a Workflow (composite 42)
Another generic management lesson disguised as an AI post.

A company gives every developer a license for an AI coding tool. They announce it in Slack and expect productivity to soar.

Instead, chaos. One dev uses it to write boilerplate and moves faster. Another uses it to refactor critical logic and quietly introduces bugs. A third ignores it completely, creating friction on the team.

They hit pause. They wrote a one-page doc. 'Use it for tests and docs. Don't use it for core business logic. Every AI-assisted merge request needs a senior review.'

Productivity actually went up. A tool is useless without a shared agreement on how to use it.

## killed [mode a] Debating Good (composite 41)
Your first management book told you this. Median.

Two members of my team spent a whole afternoon in a conference room. They were arguing over two AI-generated emails, A and B. Both looked fine to me.

One person preferred A's tone. The other liked B's structure. After an hour of this, I asked them to write down what a 'good' email actually had to accomplish. They made a checklist of five requirements.

When they measured A and B against the list, they discovered both were missing the same critical piece of information.

They stopped debating taste and wrote a better version C in ten minutes. Define 'good' before you look at the output. Your gut is not a spec.

## killed [mode b] The Demo Production Gap (composite 36)
A textbook example, which is why it feels like homework.

Imagine a team builds an AI demo that summarizes a 100-page SEC filing into three perfect bullet points. The board loves it. They rush it to production.

The first real document is a two-page customer support ticket. The AI hallucinates an entire paragraph about market trends, because it was only ever shown long, dense reports.

The demo looked for complexity. The job needed to handle simplicity. It’s a classic trap.

Before shipping, the rule is to test the magnificent cases and the mundane ones. The boring inputs are where things actually break.

## killed [mode b] The Opinion-Based Stalemate (composite 35)
You discovered the scientific method. Congratulations.

Picture this: a team has two different AI models. The PM thinks Model A is better. The lead engineer is sure Model B is superior. They argue for a week, pulling up cherry-picked examples to prove their points.

Finally, someone gets tired of the debate. She takes 20 real support tickets, runs both models, and puts the outputs side-by-side in a document. The model names are hidden.

She asks five people from other teams to just pick the better response for each ticket. The whole process takes an hour. Model A wins 18 to 2.

The argument is over. A simple side-by-side test is the fastest way to kill a week of meetings.

## killed [mode b] The Invisible AI Button (composite 34)
This is UX 101 with an 'AI' sticker on it.

Picture a team shipping a new AI assistant. They put a smart, tasteful little button in the bottom right corner of the app. It glows faintly.

And nobody clicks it.

User interviews find the problem. People don't want to stop their work, go to the magic button, and ask a question. They want help right where they are stuck.

The team removes the button. Instead, the AI offers one-sentence suggestions inside the text field the user is already typing in. Adoption triples in a week.
