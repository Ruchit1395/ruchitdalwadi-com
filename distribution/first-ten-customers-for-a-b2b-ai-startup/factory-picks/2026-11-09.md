# Factory picks, week of 2026-11-09

## SHIPPED [mode a] The project-killing spec (composite 56)
This is the closest. It's a story about people and power, not tech. The only flaw is a weak reveal.

My boss sent me a link to a slick AI video demo. His email just said: 'Can we do this for our quarterly reports? By end of month?'

Saying 'no, that's impossible' would have just made me sound negative. So I said 'yes, great idea, let's spec it out.'

I came back the next day with a one-page project plan. It included all the hidden work: manual data cleanup, a 15-step review process for each generated video, and a fact-checking stage for every sentence.

Suddenly the conversation wasn't about 'when can we have this magic?'

It was about 'is this project even worth the effort?' The project was quietly dropped. The best way to manage expectations is to make the hidden costs visible.

## SHIPPED [mode a] Hiring for the real job (composite 48)
A-tier insight and a sharp, distinct take, but it's an argument, not a parable. It lacks the winner's misdirection.

Last year, we tried to hire a 'Prompt Engineer.' The resumes were full of jargon about chain-of-thought and retrieval augmented generation.

The interviews were a disaster. We talked to ten people. Not a single one asked 'who is this for?' or 'what problem does this solve for the user?'

So we closed the job posting. We wrote a new one for 'Product Manager, AI Features.' We just looked for someone who could write a clear one-page spec.

We hired the first person we interviewed for that role. She used to be a technical writer.

Turns out, most of our 'prompt engineering' problems were just poorly defined product requirements.

## killed [mode a] Checklist-driven development (composite 45)
A great contrarian take with a strong human element, but the lack of a true reveal holds it back from outlier status.

My product manager came to me with a detailed plan for an AI onboarding agent. It would dynamically guide new hires through their paperwork, answering questions and pointing them to the right forms.

It was a neat idea. It also would have taken three months to build and test.

I asked him to do one thing first: sit with our next new hire and watch them do the paperwork. He came back an hour later and canceled the project meeting.

The new hire, a designer, had finished everything in 15 minutes with a few links from HR.

We built a simple checklist in a Google Doc instead. It shipped in an hour. Don't use three months of AI development for a problem a checklist can solve today.

## SHIPPED [mode b] The Million Dollar Demo (composite 45)
The 'COGS' payoff is killer, but the story is an impersonal illustration, not a personal narrative.

Imagine a sales team gets a demo of a new AI assistant.

For any customer question, it writes and runs a fresh database query, generates a chart, and drafts a beautiful email in seconds. The VP of Sales loves it. "Ship it. Ship it now."

The first month's bill comes in. The cost for this one feature is more than the annual salary of a senior engineer. Every "simple" question was a dozen high-cost model calls.

Nobody thought to ask what one "answer" costs. Not in engineering time, but in raw API calls at scale.

AI features are not fixed assets. They are a cost of goods sold. Model the unit economics before you write a line of code.

## killed [mode b] The Blast Radius Question (composite 45)
A perfect horror story hook, but the impersonal telling and lack of a reveal make it fall short of the winner's genius.

A team builds an AI agent to help with internal operations. Its first task: "clean up old and unused files in the shared marketing drive to save space."

The agent is very effective. It finds hundreds of files that have not been modified in 90 days. It deletes them.

This includes the entire asset library for the unlaunched Q4 campaign, which was finalized 91 days ago.

The marketing team only finds out when they go looking for the files a week later.

Before giving an agent any task, the first question is not "Can it do the job?". The first question is "What is the blast radius?". What is the absolute worst thing it can do if it misunderstands the request? Start there.

## killed [mode b] The AI Toaster Problem (composite 45)
Phenomenal payoff, but the story is so generic it feels like a business school fable, not a real war story.

Picture a company that makes a fantastic calendar app. It's simple, fast, and people love it.

A board member forwards an article titled "The AI Revolution." The new mandate comes down: "We need an AI strategy."

The team spends six months building an AI that automatically suggests meeting times. It requires connecting every calendar, email, and messaging app. It gets the time zones wrong half the time.

Users hate it. They just wanted a simple calendar.

The team did not start with a user problem. They started with a solution looking for a home. A product manager's job is often to protect users from the company's new favorite hammer.

## killed [mode b] The Surprise Log (composite 42)
The hook is funnier than its 'mode a' twin, but the third-person narrative makes it feel like a case study.

A team ships an AI that summarizes customer support tickets. The demo was flawless.

First week in production, it sees a long, angry email about a billing error. It summarizes the entire thread as: "User is upset. Probably about money."

The support lead sees this and feels his stomach drop. The engineers are embarrassed. The first instinct is to patch the prompt and pray.

The better move: start a "surprise log." Every time the AI does something weird, unexpected, or darkly funny, it goes in the log. That log is not a bug tracker. It's the seed for your first real-world evaluation set. These are the tests you cannot invent in a lab.

## killed [mode a] The AI's sea shanty (composite 40)
A charming story that fails the most important test: it announces it's about AI in the first sentence.

We built a tool to summarize customer support tickets. The first time we ran it on our own team's messages, it summarized a bug report as a sea shanty.

Everyone on the team had a good laugh. The intern, Alex, was about to delete it. I told him to save it to a text file named 'weird_outputs.log' instead.

Three weeks later, the shanty format showed up in a summary for a real customer. It was trying to describe a complex billing issue.

Because we had the log, we already had a failing test case. We had a fix deployed in an hour, not a day.

My rule now is simple: every surprising output gets logged. They aren't bugs. They're the syllabus for your next round of testing.

## killed [mode a] Ending the quality debate (composite 40)
A solid business lesson disguised as an AI post, but the structure is too straightforward to be special.

The team spent two weeks in a meeting room arguing about the prompt for our marketing copy generator. The whiteboard was a mess of words like 'witty', 'professional', and 'engaging'.

Every output was shot down. 'It's not witty enough.' 'This one sounds too robotic.' We were getting nowhere.

I got tired of it. I generated 10 pairs of outputs from two different prompts. I sent them to five salespeople and asked a simple question for each pair: 'A or B?'

They all picked from the same prompt family. We had our direction in an hour.

Stop debating 'good' in a conference room. Put two versions in front of someone who cares about the outcome and ask them to pick one.

## killed [mode b] The Slow Drift (composite 40)
An expert-level insight on a subtle problem, but the storytelling is too dry and academic to be an outlier.

Imagine an AI that categorizes news articles. For the first month after launch, it is perfect. Politics goes to politics, tech to tech.

Then, a new scientific discovery dominates the headlines for weeks. The model was never trained on this topic. It has no category for it.

So it starts getting creative. It files every article about the discovery under "Lifestyle." It does this for thousands of articles.

No alarms go off. The system is still "working." It is just quietly corrupting the entire dataset. A product manager only notices a month later when the "Lifestyle" section looks very strange.

AI systems do not always throw an error when they break. They drift. You have to monitor the outputs, not just the uptime.

## killed [mode b] Green Evals Red Inbox (composite 39)
Great tension in the hook, but the faceless protagonists make it forgettable. A lesson, not a story.

A team spends a month building an evaluation suite for their new customer service AI. It is beautiful. It covers 500 test cases, from common questions to tricky edge cases.

The model scores 98%. The dashboard is all green. They celebrate and ship it.

The next day, the support inbox is on fire. Customer complaints have tripled.

The AI is answering every question. It is just giving the wrong answer, politely. The eval suite tested for tone and clarity. It never actually checked if the answer about the return policy was correct.

An eval suite is not ground truth. It is a hypothesis about what users value. When your green dashboard disagrees with your angry users, the users are right.

## killed [mode a] The four thousand dollar bill (composite 37)
Strong hook, but the 'runaway AI bill' story is already a tired trope. Competent, not distinct.

I woke up to a bill for $4,000 from our LLM provider. The cause: an agent we had let run overnight on a list of 100 sales leads.

We had given it a clear goal: find relevant details about each company. What we never gave it was a clear stop condition.

It never decided a lead was 'researched enough'. It just kept digging deeper, following more links, and racking up API calls for hours on end for a single company.

It was doing its job, just with no sense of proportion.

Now, the first thing we define for any agent isn't the goal. It's the definition of 'done'. An agent that doesn't know when to stop is just a very fast way to burn money.

## killed [mode a] The 5,000 word prompt (composite 36)
The perfect example of a useful, competent post that is now the median AI advice; the opposite of an outlier.

I walked over to a junior engineer's desk. He had spent a week writing a system prompt that was longer than some short stories I've read. Maybe 5,000 words.

His goal was to get an LLM to generate boilerplate for a new API endpoint. But the output was inconsistent. He was trying to fix it by adding more and more instructions.

I told him to open a new file.

I deleted his entire essay. In its place, I put a single, perfect example of the finished code he wanted. That was it. No other instructions.

It worked on the first try. And the next nine. One good example did what 5,000 words of instruction could not.

## killed [mode b] Just Show The Example (composite 24)
A watered-down, impersonal version of an already median take. This is textbook content.

Picture a PM trying to get an AI to write marketing copy in a very specific format.

First, he writes a 500-word prompt explaining the tone, the structure, the exact bullet point style. The AI gets it wrong.

He adds another 500 words of clarification, with rules about what not to do. It gets it wrong in a new way.

By afternoon, the prompt is a 2,000-word instruction manual. The output is still a mess.

An engineer walks by, deletes the entire prompt, and provides a single, perfect example of the desired copy. It works instantly. The model needed a template, not a lecture.
