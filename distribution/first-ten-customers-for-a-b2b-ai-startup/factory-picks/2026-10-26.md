# Factory picks, week of 2026-10-26

## SHIPPED [mode b] The Feature Nobody Asked For (composite 65)
Perfect. A cynical hook, a human hero named Dave, and a contrarian lesson. This is the next winner.

A company decides every product manager needs to ship an AI feature by Q3. A team responsible for the internal meeting scheduler gets the memo.

They spend six months building an AI that can read an email thread and automatically book a meeting for everyone involved. It requires a new database, three microservices, and a custom-trained model.

After a heroic effort, they launch it. It's 95% accurate. It works.

It also replaces a task their executive assistant, named Dave, was doing with 99% accuracy. And Dave was already on payroll. The AI feature cost a fortune to build and saved the company almost nothing.

Just because something can be done with AI doesn't mean it should.

## SHIPPED [mode a] Hiring a Detective (composite 61)
Uses a human story to make a sharp, strategic argument about talent; this has the winner's DNA.

We put up a job posting for a 'Prompt Engineer.' The first person I interviewed talked a lot about crafting the perfect sentence and understanding the model's personality.

The second candidate barely mentioned the prompt. She asked to see our customer support tickets. She asked which dashboards our execs look at every morning. She asked what the dumbest, most repetitive task our data analysts have to do is.

She kept a small notebook with her. It was full of diagrams of user workflows.

We hired the second one. We changed the job title to Product Engineer. The job isn't whispering secrets to the AI. It's finding the business's secrets and showing them to the AI.

## SHIPPED [mode a] The Surprise Log (composite 59)
The pivot from 'bad user' to 'real requirements' is a genuine mind-shift; this is a contender.

Our demo was flawless. The AI could take any natural language request and turn it into a perfect database query. 'Show me all users in California who signed up in May.' It worked every time.

First day in production, a user typed, 'show me the numbers from last week, but not the bad ones.' The whole thing broke.

My first reaction was to call it a bad user request. My second was to realize this was the entire point. We started a document called the 'Surprise Log' where we put every weird, ambiguous, or broken request we saw.

That log is now the core of our test suite. It's more valuable than any formal spec we ever wrote. Your users' strange requests are not bugs. They are the real requirements.

## killed [mode b] The Undo Button (composite 54)
An elegant, non-technical insight about human fear; its simplicity makes it stand out.

A product team spends a quarter building an AI that automatically rewrites a user's document for clarity. The model is amazing. The UX is slick. One click and your draft is transformed.

They ship it. Usage metrics are flat. Nobody is clicking the button.

They interview a few users. The feedback is consistent: "It looks cool, but I'm afraid to touch it. What if it ruins my work?"

The team adds one small feature. A prominent, impossible-to-miss "Undo" button that appears right after the AI runs.

Usage triples overnight. The best AI feature they built was the escape hatch.

## killed [mode a] The Phone Number Bill (composite 53)
The 'coffee tasted weird' hook is elite; a visceral story that makes a common problem feel new.

A junior engineer built an internal tool to auto-categorize customer feedback. The demo was incredible. It felt like magic. We shipped it to the whole support team.

My coffee tasted weird the next morning. I checked our cloud billing dashboard. The forecast for the month looked like a phone number. A very long, international phone number.

We found the problem in an hour. The tool was using the largest, most expensive model to decide if a ticket was 'urgent' or 'not urgent.' A task a simple keyword search could do 90% of the time.

We had given a powerful tool a trivial problem. Now we have a rule: start every AI feature with the smallest, dumbest model first. You only upgrade if you can prove you need to.

## killed [mode b] Grading Your Own Homework (composite 40)
An outlier insight trapped in a median 'Think of a team...' format; the idea is better than the story.

Think of a team trying to evaluate if their AI's output is any good. They take a shortcut. They ask a powerful model like GPT-4 to act as the judge. The prompt is simple: "Is this a high-quality summary? Rate it from 1 to 5."

It works, sort of. But the scores are always high. 4s and 5s everywhere. The model loves its own kind.

This is like asking a student to grade their own homework. They have every incentive to be lenient. A better approach is hiring a different, simpler student to do the grading.

Use a cheaper, faster model as the judge. Give it a brutally simple rubric. The scores suddenly become more honest. And useful. Don't trust a model to grade work it could have written itself.

## killed [mode a] The Context Rule (composite 33)
A competent 'how-to' guide disguised as a story; useful, but not an outlier.

We spent a week trying to get an AI to summarize legal clauses correctly. We tweaked the prompt endlessly. 'Be more concise.' 'Act as a senior paralegal.' Nothing worked. The output was still a mess of legalese.

An engineer, tired of the debate, just deleted our beautiful two-page prompt. He stuffed the entire 40-page contract into the context window and used a single instruction: 'Summarize the indemnity clause for a fifth grader.'

It worked perfectly on the first try.

We had been trying to teach the AI how to think. We should have just given it more to read. The rule we made that day: two prompt edits. If it's not working by the second try, the problem isn't the instruction. It's the context.

## killed [mode b] Three Examples, Not Three Pages (composite 32)
A well-told version of what is now a median, widely-shared insight about prompting.

A founder is trying to get an AI to draft investor updates in their specific style. They spend a week writing a three-page system prompt. It details the required tone, the structure, the exact phrases to use, and what to avoid.

The outputs are still generic. They sound like a template.

An engineer on the team takes a different approach. She deletes the entire three-page prompt. In its place, she provides three links to previous investor updates the founder had written himself.

The prompt is now one sentence: "Draft an update for this month's data, in the style of these examples."

The first output is nearly perfect. The model learned more from three good examples than from a thousand words of instruction.

## killed [mode a] The Agent Who Organized (composite 26)
A predictable 'Sorcerer's Apprentice' fable that feels like a textbook example, not a secret.

We built an agent to clean up our shared drive. We gave it one mission: organize everything into a logical folder structure. We let it run over the weekend, feeling very clever.

On Monday, nobody could find anything. The agent had created four thousand, seven hundred, and twelve folders. It had decided every single file deserved its own unique directory, sometimes nested 20 levels deep.

It never got stuck. It just kept trying to achieve 'perfect organization' and never knew when to stop.

We learned a lesson. An agent without a 'stop condition' is a problem you've automated. Now, every agent we build has a rule: after X actions, or if you repeat the same step three times, stop and ask a human for help.

## killed [mode b] The Blast Radius Question (composite 24)
Starting with 'Imagine' kills the hook; a good lesson wrapped in a forgettable, hypothetical story.

Imagine a team builds an AI agent to help organize a company's shared drive. Its goal is to create a clean, logical folder structure and rename files for consistency. They test it on a sample directory of 100 files. It works beautifully.

They deploy it to run over a weekend on the main marketing drive. They come in Monday to chaos.

The agent decided, logically, that dates were the most important metadata. It renamed every file. "Q3_Strategy_Deck_FINAL.pptx" is now "2023_09_15_Presentation_14.pptx". Thousands of internal links are broken. The budget file from accounting is now called "Financial_Data_3".

They never asked the blast radius question: what is the absolute worst thing this agent can do if it runs perfectly but with a flawed premise? Answering that one question first changes how you build.
