# Factory picks, week of 2026-10-19

## SHIPPED [mode b] The Scary Magic Button (composite 64)
This is an outlier; it pivots from a tech problem to a deep human insight on trust.

A team ships a "magic document" feature. You give it a client's name, and it auto-generates a perfect quarterly report using their data. It's technically brilliant.

Users don't touch it. Adoption is zero.

They interview a few users. One of them, Brenda, points at the button. She says, "I'm terrified of that thing. What if it pulls the wrong number and sends it to my biggest client? I'd be fired." The team had given her magic. She wanted control.

The fix wasn't a better model. It was a simple workflow change. The AI drafted the report, then handed it to Brenda to review and send. A boring "approve" step made the magic useful.

## SHIPPED [mode b] The Quote of the Day (composite 63)
This is an outlier; a perfect, cynical parable about innovation theater.

Picture a company where the login page takes twelve seconds to load because of old code. Every employee waits for it ten times a day. It's a huge, invisible time sink.

That same company holds an AI hackathon. The winning project is an AI that generates a unique, inspirational quote on the login screen every day. It gets built and shipped. One of the quotes was, "The journey of a thousand miles begins with a single login."

Nobody mentions the quotes. But everyone still complains about the load time.

It's easy to get excited about building something new with AI. It is often more valuable to use it to fix something old that is quietly hurting everyone.

## killed [mode b] The Very Fast Intern (composite 63)
This is an outlier; the 'fast intern' is a world-class analogy for managing AI.

A CEO sees a demo where an AI draws a photorealistic unicorn. He turns to his product leader and says, "This is amazing. Have it invent our next product line by Friday."

This is a tough spot. You cannot say no. You also cannot deliver a magical new product line.

The PM reframes the request. She says, "Let's treat it like a very fast intern. We can't ask it to invent the future. But we can ask it to research 100 competitors, analyze 1,000 customer reviews, and find three surprising patterns by Friday. Then we can use that to find the answer."

The CEO's expectation shifts from a magic answer to a powerful analysis. The deliverable becomes possible. The best way to manage expectations for AI is to give it a job description a human could actually have.

## SHIPPED [mode a] Predicting the Future (composite 57)
The Socratic dialogue is elegant and the payoff is a powerful mental model.

My boss came to me and asked if we could use AI to predict next quarter's revenue. A simple, direct, impossible question.

Instead of saying no, I opened a spreadsheet. I showed him the last four quarters of revenue and asked him to predict the next one. He laughed. He said, 'I can't, I'd need to know about our pipeline, the new marketing campaign, and whether that big deal is closing.'

He stopped. 'Ah,' he said. 'I see.'

He answered his own question. The AI needs the same things a smart human needs. Clear inputs, not a crystal ball.

## killed [mode b] The Five-Figure Bill (composite 50)
An incredible hook and a sharp payoff almost make up for the lack of a reveal.

A startup builds an AI coding assistant. They celebrate their first thousand users. It feels like a huge win. The user feedback is great. Everyone is high-fiving.

Then the cloud bill arrives. The finance person sends the CEO a one-word email: "Hi."

It was five figures, for one month. A panicked search reveals the agent was re-reading the entire project history on every single keystroke. It was trying to be helpful. Instead, it was burning cash so fast you could feel the heat.

Cost per action isn't a problem for the finance team. It's a core product feature. If you don't design for it, your users will.

## killed [mode a] The Right Question (composite 45)
Good hiring advice with an AI skin, but not a core insight about AI itself.

We interviewed a dozen people for a senior AI role. All the resumes looked the same. A list of Python libraries, model names, and cloud services.

We hired the one person who didn't talk about any of that for the first 30 minutes.

Instead, she grilled us. What is the biggest complaint from our users? Where is our data messy? What business metric will this project move? She kept asking 'why' until she got to the root problem.

She said, 'I can't tell you which tool to use until I understand the job.' She was hired before she left the room.

## killed [mode a] The Shadow Run (composite 44)
Excellent practical advice, but it's a 'how-to' story, not a 'wow' moment.

The demo for our new AI sales-lead router was incredible. It correctly assigned every example we showed. The VP of Sales wanted it live by the end of the day.

We refused. We said we needed two weeks.

For those two weeks, we ran the AI in shadow mode. The human team kept routing leads as they always had. But for every lead, the AI would also make a decision and email it privately to the manager. No real-world impact. Just a log.

We found out the AI was misclassifying our single largest customer segment over half the time. The data was just different from the clean demo set. That shadow run saved us from a quiet disaster.

## killed [mode b] The Aggressive Ad Agent (composite 44)
A well-told disaster story whose lesson is practical but not surprising.

Imagine an autonomous agent designed to optimize a company's ad spend. It has access to the budget and the ad platform APIs.

A marketer, trying to hit a quarterly goal, gives it a new instruction: "Be more aggressive."

The team was in an all-hands meeting about company values when it happened. The agent interpreted "aggressive" as "bid on every keyword related to our industry, no matter the cost." It drained the entire quarterly marketing budget in 90 minutes.

Before you give an agent a goal, you have to define its guardrails. Ask one question: what's the absolute worst this can do if it completely misunderstands me? Then write the code to prevent that.

## killed [mode a] The Rogue Archivist (composite 42)
A strong cautionary tale, but it's a straightforward lesson, not a perspective shift.

We built an internal AI agent to help us clean up old projects in our task tracker. We let it run for the first time on a Tuesday night.

On Wednesday morning, we found it had archived half of our active Q3 initiatives. The reason in the logs: 'project description lacks sufficient detail for goal synthesis.' It just kept going, action after action.

We spent the whole day restoring everything from backups. A total mess.

Now, every agent we build has a handbrake. Three consecutive low-confidence actions, and it has to stop and ping a human. We learned the most important thing to teach an agent is when to quit.

## killed [mode a] The Prompt in Git (composite 39)
A sharp, practical lesson for engineers that's too niche to be a true outlier.

I had two engineers building an AI feature. For a week, their results were totally inconsistent. One day it worked, the next it was broken. They were blaming the model.

I dug in and found the problem. They were both editing the same system prompt in a shared Google Doc. One would add a sentence to fix an edge case. The other would remove it an hour later, thinking it was hurting performance. It was silent, invisible sabotage.

We fixed it in ten minutes. We created a prompt.txt file in our code repository. From that day on, any change to the prompt had to go through a pull request, just like any other code.

Prompts aren't just instructions. They're configuration. They need version control.

## killed [mode a] The Argument Room (composite 36)
A useful process tip that feels more like good management than a viral insight.

My team was stuck. We were building an AI to generate marketing copy, and every review meeting turned into an argument. 'This one feels flat.' 'I don't like the tone of that one.' It was all subjective, all vibes.

Progress stalled for a week. So we paused.

We locked ourselves in a room with a whiteboard. We didn't look at any AI outputs. Instead, we defined what 'good' meant. We created a simple four-item checklist. Does it mention the key feature? Is it under 280 characters? Does it have a clear call to action? Is the tone optimistic?

Suddenly, the arguments stopped. It wasn't about opinions anymore. It was about whether the output passed the test. We had a spec.

## killed [mode a] The Two Dollar Summary (composite 31)
A solid cost-saving tip that lacks the surprise of an outlier.

Our new AI document summary feature was a huge hit. Everyone loved it. Then we got the first monthly bill. It was costing us nearly two dollars every time a user clicked 'summarize'.

We were about to start rationing access. We felt sick.

Then someone in the team meeting asked a dumb question. 'What if the doc already has a summary?' We checked. Almost 70% of our internal docs had a 'TLDR' section right at the top.

We added one line of code. If a TLDR exists, display it. Don't call the AI. The bill vanished.

## killed [mode b] One Example Beats Ten (composite 28)
A more vivid retelling of the same common knowledge from candidate [0].

Picture a team spending a week trying to get a model to output clean JSON. They rewrite the prompt endlessly. Be concise. Use this exact schema. Do not add commentary. Do not forget the closing bracket.

Every day, it sort of works. Then it breaks again, spitting out a novel instead of a data object. Morale is low. They almost scrapped the feature on Tuesday.

Then one engineer deletes half the instructions. In their place, she adds a single, perfect example of the JSON they want. Just one.

The model's compliance becomes nearly 100% overnight. They didn't need a better instruction manual. They needed a map.

## killed [mode a] The Ten Page Prompt (composite 24)
The canonical 'few-shot prompting' story; competent but completely overused.

We had an AI task to categorize user feedback. We spent two weeks writing a ten page prompt. A masterpiece of instructions, edge cases, and definitions. It was very clever.

It was also wrong 40% of the time.

A junior engineer, on her first day, saw our mess. She deleted the whole thing. She replaced it with three perfect examples of feedback and the correct category for each one.

That's it. Just three examples. The accuracy jumped to 95%.

We had tried to teach the AI with a dictionary. She taught it by showing it a picture.
