# Factory picks, week of 2026-10-12

## SHIPPED [mode a] The Self-Driving Intern (composite 64)
This is the one. It has the same absurd, high-stakes DNA as the winner.

We hired a brilliant intern. On his first day, we gave him a company credit card and a goal: 'optimize our cloud spend.'

He got to work immediately. He found a cheaper vendor, signed a new contract, and migrated our entire production database. He did it all overnight to minimize disruption.

The site was down for eight hours.

We are building AI agents and giving them the same combination of a clear goal and far too much authority. We focus on making them smart. We forget to give them spending limits, mandatory human approvals, and a big red stop button.

## SHIPPED [mode b] Shouting At The Tourist (composite 59)
The reveal and payoff are genius; a truly paradigm-shifting take on prompting.

A man is trying to give directions to a tourist. He says, loudly, "TURN LEFT AT THE BIG TREE." The tourist looks confused. So he repeats himself, slower. "TURN. LEFT. AT. THE. BIG. TREE."

It doesn't work. Then a friend walks up, pulls out a pen, and draws a simple map on a napkin. The tourist smiles and walks away.

This is how we debug prompts. We rephrase the same instruction ten different ways, just louder and slower. The model doesn't need better adjectives. It needs a map.

Stop rewording the command. Add context. Show it one perfect example of what you want. A good example is the map that makes the words make sense.

## SHIPPED [mode a] The Library Card (composite 58)
Excellent. The 'library card' is a sticky, distinct metaphor for RAG.

A junior support hire was struggling. He couldn't answer customer questions fast enough. We gave him better scripts. We taught him new phrases. Nothing worked.

He was about to quit. In a last attempt, we just gave him read-only access to our internal engineering wiki. A huge, messy library of product specs and bug reports.

Overnight, he became our best agent.

We keep trying to fix our AI with better prompts and clever instructions. We're polishing the script. Most of the time, what the AI really needs is just a library card. Access to better information beats a better-phrased question.

## killed [mode a] The Coffee Shop Economics (composite 54)
A visceral and memorable analogy for the cost/latency trap. Very strong.

We opened a coffee shop. We bought a $20,000 espresso machine that used a special multi-phase pressure cycle. It took five minutes to pull one shot. But it was the best espresso in the city.

Our first ten customers were evangelists. They loved it.

Then a line of 50 people formed on Saturday. We couldn't serve them fast enough. People left. The business failed a month later.

This is the standard playbook for building an AI feature. Find the most powerful, expensive model to nail the demo for ten users. Then act surprised when the cost and latency make the business completely unviable at any real scale.

## killed [mode a] The Two Carpenters (composite 50)
A sharp, timely take that correctly values systems over artistry.

I needed a table built. I interviewed two people.

The first was a master craftsman. He could carve intricate details by hand with a chisel. A true artist.

The second was a decent carpenter. But she was an expert with a nail gun, a laser cutter, and a CNC machine. She knew how to operate the systems.

The first one could have made a beautiful piece of art. The second one could build my 100 tables.

For most AI work, we hire for the first person, the prompt artist. We should be hiring for the second. The person who can build a repeatable system is more valuable than the one who can write a perfect sentence.

## killed [mode a] The Smart Hammer (composite 49)
A+ hook and metaphor, but the payoff is a generic 'don't over-engineer' platitude.

My friend bought a new 'smart hammer.' It had a tiny screen on it that showed nail velocity and trajectory analytics. He paid $300 for it.

He used it to hang one picture frame in his living room. It took him ten minutes because he kept checking the screen.

I used my old $10 hammer to hang a similar frame. It took me thirty seconds.

We are obsessed with smart hammers right now. Every product meeting includes the question, 'how can we add AI to this?' We're so focused on the tool that we forget the simple nail. The best solution is often the one that doesn't need a battery.

## killed [mode b] The Intern With a Credit Card (composite 48)
A hypothetical, weaker version of #8. The story with a disastrous outcome is the outlier.

Imagine hiring a new intern. On day one, you give them a company credit card with no limit, the keys to the server room, and a single instruction: "Improve our business."

You don't give them a budget. You don't give them a supervisor. You don't tell them what not to do. Then you walk away for a week.

We are all building this intern and calling it an "AI agent." We give it powerful tools and a vague goal, then act surprised when it does something unpredictable and expensive.

Don't give an agent a credit card and a dream. Give it a budget, an approval workflow, and a clear list of fireable offenses. Define its blast radius before you let it run.

## killed [mode b] The Free Appetizer Problem (composite 42)
A solid business parable, but less specific and visceral than the coffee shop.

A new restaurant offers free gourmet appetizers to its first ten guests. The reviews are spectacular. Five stars. Word spreads.

On week two, they offer the free appetizer to every customer. The place is packed. By Friday, they are bankrupt.

This is the unit cost trap of every AI product. The price of running the most powerful model for a handful of internal demos is a rounding error. The cost of running it for 10,000 active users is a board-level problem.

The goal isn't the most impressive demo. It's a sustainable product. Model your cost-per-query on day one, not after the first giant bill arrives.

## killed [mode b] The AI Nail Gun (composite 42)
A good visual for a tired 'right tool for the job' argument.

Think of a construction crew that gets a powerful new nail gun. It's fast and impressive. So they use it for everything.

They frame walls with it. They install windows with it. They hang drywall with it. They even connect the plumbing pipes with it. The house goes up in record time. Then it rains.

AI is that new nail gun. We're so excited by what it can do that we are not asking what it should do. Applying it everywhere creates a product that looks finished in a demo but falls apart under real pressure.

The right question isn't "Can we use AI for this?" It's "Is AI the best tool for this job?" Sometimes, you need a screw. Not everything is a nail.

## killed [mode a] The Grumpy Intern (composite 33)
A great human detail (the angry intern), but zero reveal.

We had two AI models generating marketing headlines. We couldn't agree on which one was better.

We printed out 100 examples and gave them to our summer intern. We told him to score them from 1 to 5. His ratings were completely random.

I asked him what was wrong. He slammed the papers down. 'I don't know what you want. Is 'good' supposed to be clever or clear? Funny or professional?'

He was right. We ask our automated evals to judge quality all the time, but we give them the same vague instructions. If a human intern can't score without a clear rubric, an LLM-as-judge can't either.

## killed [mode a] The Chef's Order (composite 28)
A generic product management anecdote with 'AI' pasted on top.

My CEO walked up to my desk. He said, 'I want you to build something amazing with AI. Surprise me.'

So we spent a month building a tool that could write beautiful, complex legal clauses. We thought it was magic.

We showed it to him. He was confused. 'This is nice,' he said. 'But can it just check for typos?' He wanted a cheeseburger and we had prepared a twelve-course tasting menu.

'Make it magic' is not a product spec. It's our job to translate executive excitement into a concrete order. Ask what the simplest, most useful version looks like before you start building the future.

## killed [mode a] The Silent Button (composite 24)
A product update, not a story. The total lack of a reveal kills it.

We built an AI summary button for our main dashboard. It took a month. It was clever, accurate, and wrote a perfect recap of a user's weekly performance.

We shipped it. Nothing happened. Flat metrics.

I called a customer and asked why he didn't use it. He said, 'I don't trust it. What if it's wrong and I send a bad number to my boss? I don't know how it got the answer.'

We thought we were shipping a feature. What we actually needed to ship was the receipt. The proof. The user needed to see the work so they could trust the answer.

## killed [mode a] The Monday Test (composite 22)
The most common AI analogy; the definition of median content.

We hired a new analyst. We spent a week training him on perfectly clean spreadsheets from last quarter. He was flawless. A machine.

On Monday, we gave him a live feed from the sales team's CRM. It was a mess of typos, empty fields, and notes typed with one thumb in a parking lot.

The analyst froze. He couldn't make sense of it.

We do this with every AI model. We train it on a perfect demo dataset. Then we're shocked when it collapses under the weight of a normal Monday morning. The model isn't the problem. The training is.
