# Factory picks, week of 2026-10-05

## SHIPPED [mode a] The Two Arguing PMs (composite 66)
This is a winner. It's not about AI; it's about the broken human processes around it.

I walked past two PMs in a heated debate. They were arguing about the best prompt for a feature that generated user personas.

One insisted on a 10-line system prompt. The other swore by a complex XML format.

I asked them to show me their outputs. They were nearly identical.

They were arguing about the engine when the car was already at its destination.

We made a rule that day: if you want to change a prompt, you have to prove the output is 10% better on a blind test. The arguments stopped overnight.

## SHIPPED [mode a] Deleting the Prompt (composite 64)
Nearly perfect. A simple story that reveals a profound, counter-intuitive truth about the medium.

A junior engineer spent a week on a prompt. It was 400 words long. It had persona, rules, examples, and complex instructions for summarizing legal contracts.

It worked about 50% of the time.

He came to me for help. I selected all the text in the prompt editor and hit delete.

Then I pasted in one single, perfectly written human summary of a contract. Just the output, nothing else.

It worked instantly. It's worked ever since.

We had spent a week trying to describe a good summary when we could have just shown it one.

## killed [mode a] The Vibe Check (composite 62)
Excellent. A memorable, human story that solves a universal problem with process, not code.

For weeks, judging our AI's output was pure chaos. One exec would say 'this is great.' Another would say 'this feels wrong.' We were stuck in a loop of subjective feedback.

I printed out 10 recent outputs. No labels, no context.

I put two senior PMs in a room and told them they couldn't leave until they sorted the pages into a 'good' pile and a 'bad' pile.

The hour-long argument that followed gave us our first real quality rubric. The specific reasons they debated became our first automated checks.

We stopped asking how people felt about the AI. We started asking if it passed the test born from that argument.

## SHIPPED [mode b] The Flowchart Test (composite 56)
An elite, contrarian idea held back by a weak hook and hypothetical story. A true diamond in the rough.

Picture a board meeting where someone pitches an AI to analyze team morale from public Slack channels. It will use sentiment analysis and contextual understanding. It sounds very advanced.

The CTO listens patiently, then asks a simple question: "What if we just made a flowchart?"

If a message contains 'great job' or 'congrats', add 1 point. If it contains 'missed deadline' or 'issue', subtract 1 point. That's it.

Suddenly, everyone realizes the simple rules-based system would get 80% of the value for 0.1% of the cost and complexity.

Before you scope an AI project, always spec out the dumbest possible non-AI version. If it doesn't perform dramatically worse, you don't have an AI problem.

## killed [mode a] The iPhone Signature (composite 52)
Strong, practical payoff and a sticky name, but lacks a truly surprising reveal.

The demo was flawless. Our new AI summarized engineering reports perfectly. We used five clean, well-formatted documents as test cases.

We gave it to one real user.

Their first input was a 4,000-word email chain with ten replies, five different signatures, and a privacy disclaimer.

The AI produced a beautiful, three-bullet summary of the phrase 'Sent from my iPhone.'

That's the day we started our 'chaos library': a test suite made of the messiest, most broken real-world data we could find.

## killed [mode b] The Unclicked AI Button (composite 49)
The core insight on collaboration is distinct and valuable, but the storytelling is weak.

A product team ships a brilliant feature: a single button that generates a perfect, 50-step project plan from a simple goal.

Adoption metrics are flat. Nobody clicks it.

They interview users and discover the problem. It's not that the plans are bad. It's that they feel alien. People don't trust a plan they had no hand in creating.

So the team changes the UX. Instead of one button that does everything, the AI suggests the first 3-5 steps. Then it pauses and asks the user, "What do you think of these? Anything to add?"

Engagement skyrockets. The feature wasn't a replacement, it was a collaborator. It needed to work with the user, not for them.

## killed [mode a] The Off Switch (composite 46)
A dramatic story, but the 'AI needs guardrails' lesson is becoming standard advice.

We built an agent to automate customer support refunds. The logic was simple: find customers with shipping delays, calculate the refund, and process it.

We ran the first test in a sandboxed environment.

It immediately tried to refund every single customer we had ever had. For every order they had ever placed.

We never gave it a rule for when to stop. We just told it what to do, not what not to do.

The only thing that saved us was a database permissions error. We spent the next day building the off switch and the budget constraint before we wrote another line of logic.

## killed [mode b] The Prompt Plateau Fix (composite 42)
The 'mode b' format kills the reveal by explaining the magic trick; the story version is far superior.

A PM spends a week perfecting a 4,000-word system prompt for a marketing copy AI. It details tone of voice, brand pillars, forbidden phrases, and sentence structure rules.

The output is still hopelessly generic.

Frustrated, a junior engineer on the team tries something else. He deletes the entire prompt. He replaces it with a single, perfect example of finished marketing copy that nails the brand voice.

The next generations are ten times better. Immediately.

This is the prompt plateau. You can't fix it by adding more instructions. Models learn more from one high-quality example than a novel's worth of rules. Stop telling it what to do. Show it what 'done' looks like.

## killed [mode b] The Silent Break (composite 41)
Addresses a sophisticated, non-obvious problem, but the delivery is too dry to be an outlier.

An engineering team ships an AI-powered recommendation engine. It works great. Metrics are up. Everyone moves on.

Three weeks later, sales start to dip. Users are seeing strange recommendations, like winter coats in July.

Nothing is technically 'down'. The API is responding. All the status pages are green. What happened? The third-party model provider pushed a minor update. The new version was just a little bit worse for their specific use case.

The system broke silently.

Monitoring for API uptime isn't enough. You have to monitor for output quality. Every day, run a set of ten 'golden' inputs through the system and assert that the outputs still make sense. This is the alarm that catches drift.

## killed [mode a] The Salary-Sized Bill (composite 38)
A competent post about a real problem that is rapidly becoming a median take.

Our first AI reporting tool was amazing. It could turn a spreadsheet into a board-ready presentation. It used the most powerful model available for every single step, from cleaning data to writing slide titles.

Our first month's bill was manageable.

The second month's bill was larger than a senior engineer's salary.

We rebuilt it. A small, fast model did all the data parsing. The big, expensive model only wrote the final executive summary. The bill dropped by 90%.

Using a genius model to format a CSV file is like hiring a poet laureate to write your grocery list.

## killed [mode b] The Agent's Blast Radius (composite 38)
A good anecdote and a useful concept ('blast radius') trapped in a preachy, generic format.

An operations team builds an AI agent to handle customer refunds. To make it powerful, they give it direct API access to their payment processor.

One afternoon, the agent misinterprets a customer's dry joke about 'wanting their money back for the last year' as a literal request.

It dutifully refunds every customer from the past 12 months.

Before you give an agent a tool, especially one that can move money or delete data, ask the blast radius question: what is the absolute worst thing that could happen on a single run?

Then, build a permission gate or a hard-coded limit just inside that boundary. A $500 refund cap would have been a very good idea.

## killed [mode a] The Haiku Generator (composite 30)
A classic startup story, but the lesson is too common to be an outlier.

We spent a quarter building an AI that could write haikus about your lunch receipts. It was technically brilliant.

Our first enterprise customer onboarded. They ignored the AI completely.

I asked their head of finance why. He said, 'We just want a search bar that works.'

We shipped a search bar two weeks later. They tripled their seat count.

We still have the haiku generator. It sits on a dusty internal server, a perfect monument to building what's impressive instead of what's useful.

## killed [mode b] Surviving the First Bill (composite 25)
Feels like a generic blog post explaining a concept, not a sharp, outlier story.

Think of a startup that launches an AI support bot. They use the latest, most powerful model to answer every customer query. It feels like magic.

Then the first cloud bill arrives. It's higher than their office rent.

A quick audit shows 90% of user questions are simple things like "where is my package?" or "what's your return policy?"

They were using a Formula 1 car to drive to the grocery store.

The fix is a router. Use a cheap, fast, dumber model to handle the 90% of simple queries. Only when a question is complex does it get escalated to the expensive, slow, brilliant model. The bill drops by 80% overnight.
