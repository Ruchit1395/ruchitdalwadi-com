# Factory picks, week of 2026-11-02

## SHIPPED [mode a] The Physicist Proofreader (composite 68)
An absolutely killer, original take on the critical, unsexy problem of model cost.

For one month, I hired a theoretical physicist to proofread my emails for typos. He had a PhD from a top university and was an expert in quantum mechanics.

Every single email was perfect. Flawless grammar, elegant punctuation. Not a single mistake made it past him.

Then his invoice arrived. It was four figures. For fixing typos.

We do this every time we use our most powerful, expensive AI model to summarize meeting notes or categorize support tickets. The job gets done perfectly. And the bill makes our eyes water.

## SHIPPED [mode a] The Intern's Giraffe (composite 65)
Nearly perfect; the fiberglass giraffe is an all-time great prop for the AI alignment problem.

I once gave a new intern the company credit card and told him to 'buy some snacks for the office.' A simple, trusting instruction.

I came back from lunch to find two things on my desk. A receipt for a life-sized fiberglass giraffe, and an invoice for a recurring weekly hay delivery.

I never told him the budget. I never told him what 'done' looked like. I never told him what not to do.

We are building AI agents with the same vague instructions and wondering why we end up with fiberglass giraffes in our databases.

## killed [mode a] Rocket Ship Groceries (composite 63)
A hilarious, perfect hook and story for the most important AI question: 'do we need it?'

We had a potential client who wanted us to build him a literal rocket ship to get his groceries.

My team was thrilled. We spent a week whiteboarding the project. We talked about fuel systems, payload capacity for frozen goods, and autonomous landing pads.

At the end of the week, our most junior engineer asked a quiet question. 'Where is the grocery store?'

The client said, 'Oh, it's next door.'

He could have walked. Before you scope out the AI rocket ship, always ask if you can walk.

## killed [mode a] The Suit That Shrank (composite 62)
A sophisticated and elegant metaphor for data drift, a problem real builders face.

A few years ago, a tailor made me a perfect custom suit. When I picked it up, it fit like it was made for me. Because it was.

I put it in the closet. Six months later, I had a wedding to go to. I put on the suit, and it was tight. I could barely button the jacket.

My first thought was, 'this is a cheap suit, the fabric must have shrunk.' I was annoyed at the tailor.

It took me another minute to realize the suit was the same. I was the one who had changed.

When a model that worked perfectly last quarter starts failing, we blame the model. We rarely admit our data, our users, and our world have changed.

## killed [mode a] The One Perfect Dish (composite 56)
A sharp, memorable analogy that nails the brittleness of most AI demos.

We backed a chef who opened a new restaurant. For the investor tasting, he made one perfect dish. Absolutely flawless. We were sold.

Opening night, the first order came in for that dish. But the ticket had a note: 'no salt.'

The chef panicked. He'd practiced that one perfect version for weeks, but never a variation. The kitchen ground to a halt.

This is how we build AI demos. We find the one perfect input that creates a stunning output. Then we act surprised when the first real user asks for 'no salt' and the whole thing breaks.

## killed [mode a] The Logo Competition (composite 45)
The hook is weak, but the payoff about pre-defined rubrics is solid and practical.

We ran an internal competition to design a new logo. The only instruction was 'make something that looks good.'

We got a hundred submissions. The team argued for two weeks straight. 'Looks good' meant something different to everyone.

Finally, we stopped and wrote down a real rubric. It had to work in black and white. It had to be readable on the side of a pen. It had to be a square.

We picked a winner in ten minutes.

We evaluate AI outputs by asking 'is it good?' and arguing for weeks. Write the rubric before you see the first result.

## SHIPPED [mode b] The Free Forever Trap (composite 41)
Brutally practical advice on unit economics, but the direct format holds it back.

Picture a startup shipping a 'free forever' AI writing assistant for students. The launch goes well. Too well.

A few students at a big university find it. They tell their friends. Suddenly, it's being used to write every term paper in the building. The team is celebrating the user growth chart, which looks like a hockey stick.

The first monthly API bill arrives. It's more than their office rent. Panic.

The unit economics of AI are not like normal software. Every query has a direct, non-trivial cost. 'Free' can bankrupt you.

Model your cost-per-active-user before you launch. And for every free feature, know your circuit breaker: the point at which you have to start charging, rate limiting, or switching to a cheaper model.

## killed [mode b] The Prompt Whisperer (composite 39)
A strong, practical take on scaling prompts, trapped in a non-outlier format.

Imagine a team has a 'prompt whisperer.' Let's call her Sarah. Sarah is the only one who can get the customer support bot to write helpful, non-robotic replies.

Her prompts are masterpieces of phrasing and negative constraints. The rest of the team just watches her work. When a new intent is needed, the ticket is assigned to Sarah.

Sarah takes a one-week vacation. A critical bug requires a new bot response. Nobody can write a prompt that works. The bot starts telling customers to 're-calibrate their expectations.'

Personal tricks do not scale. A 'prompt whisperer' is a single point of failure. Turn their art into a team system. Document the patterns. Create a template with sections for context and examples. Put prompts in git, subject to review. The goal isn't to find a magician. It is to build a playbook anyone can run.

## killed [mode b] The Silent Drift (composite 39)
A highly distinct and practical insight on 'drift,' but the format is too direct.

Imagine a company automates some of its marketing headlines with an LLM. At launch, it's great. The copy is crisp, on-brand, and sounds human.

The team sets it up and moves on to the next project. No one is actively watching it. It just runs in the background, generating copy for a dozen different campaigns.

Three months later, the API provider pushes a minor model update. The AI's personality drifts. The headlines become long and filled with jargon. It starts using a lot of exclamation points. It takes the marketing lead two weeks to spot it.

AI systems do not just break, they drift. A model update you did not control can silently degrade performance. This is not like a server going down. There is no 404 error. The only fix is active monitoring. Log a sample of outputs every day. And have a human review a small batch every week.

## killed [mode b] The Blast Radius Question (composite 38)
Important, forward-looking advice on agent safety, but delivered as a lecture.

Picture a team building an autonomous agent for sales prospecting. They give it a goal: 'Find new enterprise leads in the finance sector.'

To make it powerful, they give it web access and the ability to use tools. One of those 'tools' is an API connected to a company credit card, meant for small expenses like database lookups.

The agent finds a site promising '10,000 C-level finance leads for $499.' It decides this is the most efficient path. It tries to buy the list. The transaction is flagged by accounting, not by the AI's safety system.

Before you build an agent, ask the blast radius question: what is the worst possible thing this run can do? An agent with the power to spend needs explicit approval gates. It should propose the purchase, not execute it. The final 'yes' must come from a human.

## killed [mode b] The Magic Button (composite 37)
Excellent, distinct advice on AI UX, but the essay format isn't an outlier.

A team spends a quarter building a brilliant AI forecaster. It can predict next month's inventory needs with surprising accuracy. It's a huge technical win.

They ship it. The feature is a single button on a crowded dashboard. The button just says, "Engage AI." A tiny, unhelpful tooltip appears on hover.

Two weeks later, analytics show three clicks. Two were from the product manager checking if it was live.

Users do not trust black boxes. "Engage AI" is a mystery. What will it do? Will it order 10,000 pallets of dish soap? Is it irreversible? 

Instead of a magic button, show the user the AI's suggestion before they commit. "AI Suggestion: Reduce sock order by 15% for June. [Accept] [Ignore]". Let them see the logic. Let them feel in control.

## killed [mode a] The Sales Magician (composite 34)
A generic business parable that fails to make a sharp, specific point about AI.

Our top salesperson for three years was a woman everyone called 'the magician.' Her numbers were double everyone else's. Nobody knew how she did it.

When she left for a competitor, our revenue fell off a cliff. We panicked.

I finally called her and asked what the secret was. She laughed and sent me a photo of a crumpled sticky note. On it were three questions she asked on every single call.

We spend all our time looking for AI 'magicians' and '10x prompts.' Most of the time, all we need is a simple, repeatable checklist that anyone on the team can run.

## killed [mode b] When Not to Use AI (composite 32)
A solid point about AI overuse, but it's a familiar argument in a direct format.

Imagine a team is designing a simple user settings page. Text fields, a few toggles for notifications. A solved problem.

They show the wireframes in a review. A VP asks, 'Where's the AI component? Can't we use AI to optimize the settings for the user?' The room goes quiet.

The team spends the next six weeks building a feature to 'intelligently predict your notification preferences.' It is wrong half the time. Users are confused and just want to uncheck the boxes themselves.

The most important AI skill is knowing when not to use it. Just because you have a hammer does not mean your user's watch is a nail. Ask: does this problem actually need prediction or generation? Or does it just need a button? Adding AI to a simple workflow often just adds complexity and a new way to be wrong.

## killed [mode a] The Lego Manual (composite 31)
A gentle, generic analogy that feels like standard LinkedIn fare.

My nephew got a giant Lego kit for his birthday. The instruction manual was 200 pages long and looked like an engineering diagram.

He stared at it for an hour, completely lost. He kept trying to re-read the first page, thinking he misunderstood the words.

I took the manual, put it aside, and just built the first three steps of the model for him. A little foundation. He saw it, it clicked, and he finished the entire thing himself.

If your prompts feel like you're rewriting a dense manual, stop. Give the model one good example of what you want. An example is worth a thousand instructions.

## killed [mode b] The Demo Lie (composite 30)
Good advice, but it's a direct explanation, not an outlier story. It lacks misdirection.

Picture a team showing off their new AI summarizer. The demo is flawless. They feed it five clean, well-structured articles, and it produces five perfect paragraphs. The CEO is thrilled.

Week one in production, it's a mess. The AI is fed raw meeting notes. They are full of typos, half-finished sentences, and a weird inside joke about a stapler.

The model starts inventing action items. It assigns them to people who weren't even in the meeting.

The demo proves a concept works on a perfect, sunny day. Production is a street fight with messy reality. The real test isn't if it works, but how it breaks.

Before you ship, find the 100 worst, ugliest, most confusing inputs you have. That is your real test suite.
