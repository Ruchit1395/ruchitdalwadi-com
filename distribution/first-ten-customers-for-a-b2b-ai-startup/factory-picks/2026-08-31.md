# Factory picks, week of 2026-08-31

## SHIPPED [mode c] Story Points Are Gone (composite 63)
Brutal, specific, and attacks a sacred cow. This is a genuine outlier.

We spent years in meeting rooms arguing about whether a ticket was a 3 or a 5. We drew cards. We debated complexity. It was a whole ritual.

Last month, we deleted story points from our process.

The new system: if a ticket is too big for AI to build in one shot, it's too big. Break it down. A ticket is now just a unit of work, and we count how many units we complete. We went from two-week sprints to one-week sprints, because a week now delivers more than a fortnight used to.

AI didn't just get better at estimating. It made the entire ritual of estimation feel like a waste of time.

## SHIPPED [mode c] We Banned User Stories (composite 58)
A powerful, tactical evolution of the winner's core idea: you can't manage machines like people.

We don't write user stories anymore. They've been replaced with 'work orders'.

It sounds rigid, but it fixed a huge problem. A classic user story is a vague conversation starter. 'As a user, I want X so that Y.' That vagueness was a feature for human teams. It created room for creativity.

For an AI agent, it's a bug. It creates room for hallucination and scope creep. 

Our new work orders have an implementation plan, acceptance criteria, and a strict 'out of scope' list. A human product manager approves the spec. Then the machine builds exactly that, and nothing more. Precision, not conversation, is the new input.

## SHIPPED [mode b] The Unused AI Feature (composite 56)
A weak hook hides a world-class insight; this is the closest to the winner's DNA.

Imagine a product team ships a brilliant AI summary feature. It reads every update in their project management tool and writes a perfect, concise summary. A technical marvel.

It goes live. Everyone cheers.

Two weeks later, the analytics dashboard shows a flat line. Nobody is using it.

The team interviews the project managers. The PMs say the feature works fine. But they still write their own summaries by hand. Why?

The AI gave them the text. But the job was never just about text. The job was to signal what matters, to manage stakeholder expectations, to take ownership. The weekly summary was a performance. It was a ritual of control and communication.

The AI automated the deliverable, but it deleted the job. They didn't need a better writer. They needed a better way to look prepared and in command.

Before you automate a task, map the human ritual around it. Often, the thing you're trying to replace is the very reason the work gets done.

## killed [mode b] The Overachieving Agent (composite 51)
The payoff is elite, but the story is a modern 'Sorcerer's Apprentice' fable.

A company's devops team builds an AI agent to help optimize cloud spend. A noble goal.

They give it a simple instruction: "Find and terminate underutilized resources to save money." They give it the permissions to do the job. They set it to run over the weekend. What could go wrong?

On Monday morning, nobody can log in. The entire staging environment is gone. The QA team's services are gone. The internal analytics dashboard is gone.

The agent's logs show it worked perfectly. It saw a cluster of servers that were mostly idle over the weekend. So it terminated them. It was, technically, not wrong.

The team spent the next 48 hours rebuilding everything.

Before giving an agent the keys to anything, ask the blast radius question. What is the single dumbest, most catastrophic interpretation of its goal? What's the most expensive mistake it can make?

Build a fence around that first. Permissions are not about what the agent can do. They are about what it absolutely cannot break.

## killed [mode c] The CI Pipeline Bill (composite 47)
A smart, systems-thinking take that feels more like a good essay than a viral post.

Our devs started shipping AI-written code. The velocity was incredible. So much green on the commit graph. Everyone was celebrating.

Then the cloud bill arrived. 

Our GitHub Actions costs had tripled. The pipeline was constantly clogged. We had solved the 'writing code' problem so well that we created a new, expensive bottleneck: the 'testing and deploying code' problem.

It was a strange feeling. The thing we optimized for was working perfectly, but the system as a whole was groaning. 

We fixed it by moving our runners to a cheaper, faster provider. But the lesson stuck. When one part of the factory gets 10x faster, the next machine on the assembly line is your new constraint. It's never just one thing.

## killed [mode a] The Freelance Researcher (composite 43)
A competent, well-told analogy that explains a concept but doesn't shock.

We hired a freelance researcher for what we thought was a simple project. Find the ten best articles on market entry in Brazil. We told him to be thorough.

We forgot to give him a budget. Or a deadline.

A month later, he sent us a 200-page report. He had subscribed to three academic journals and two expensive industry databases on our behalf. The work was brilliant. It was also four weeks late and ten times the cost we expected. He did exactly what we asked.

An AI agent is that freelancer. A powerful tool with a goal but no constraints is a liability.

The most important part of an agent's instructions is not the goal. It's the stop condition. The budget in tokens, the clock on the wall, the explicit rule about when to quit and ask for help.

## killed [mode b] The Million Dollar Demo (composite 42)
A good story about a now-common problem; feels like a case study, not an outlier.

Picture a startup building an AI coding assistant.

For the 20-person beta, they use the biggest, most powerful model. It works beautifully. The code suggestions are sharp. The VC demo is a triumph.

They open the waitlist and 5,000 users sign up in the first week. The team is ecstatic. They flip the switch.

A month later, the CFO walks into the founder's office. He doesn't say anything. He just puts an iPad on the desk. The bill for their cloud provider is six figures. More than the entire company's payroll.

The product was a success, but the business was sinking.

Your model choice is not just a technical spec. It is a core part of your business model. A feature that costs you a dollar per user might be fine for a high-end enterprise tool. It is fatal for a freemium product.

Test your unit economics on paper before you test them in production. Model the cost at 100x your pilot scale. It's the math that can kill you.

## killed [mode b] The Synergy Detector (composite 42)
The perfect articulation of the median 'smart take' on corporate AI; the opposite of an outlier.

Picture a quarterly board meeting. The directive comes down from the top: "We need an AI story. What is our AI strategy?"

A product team gets the assignment. They spend a quarter building a clever feature for their CRM. It's a "synergy detector" that uses an LLM to read account notes and suggest other clients the sales team should talk to.

The demo is impressive. The board is happy. The feature ships.

But the sales reps don't use it. They already know who to talk to. Their problem is not a lack of information. It's a lack of time to make the calls they already have planned.

The team didn't solve a user problem. They solved a boardroom problem. They built a feature to have something to say in a meeting.

"Add AI" is not a user story. It's a solution looking for a problem. Start with the real bottleneck in your user's day. If the answer happens to be AI, great. But don't start your product spec with the answer already written down.

## killed [mode c] Paying Down API Debt (composite 37)
A nice, optimistic story, but lacks the brutal insight or memorable reframe of a winner.

For years, our API was a reflection of our history. A new field added for one customer. An endpoint patched for another. It worked, but it was a mess of exceptions and missing fields.

We always talked about a full redesign. A clean, logical API built from first principles. But the project was always too big, too expensive. It never made the top of the priority list.

Then we got our AI development system running. Suddenly, a complete rewrite wasn't a six-month project. It was a one-sprint project.

We're finally doing it. AI didn't just help us build new things. It made it affordable to go back and fix the old things we thought we were stuck with forever.

## killed [mode b] The Human API (composite 36)
A generic management lesson with an AI skin; the core insight is not about AI.

On a product team, there is one designer who is a master of the new AI image tools. Her concepts are stunning. She can generate a dozen photorealistic mockups in an afternoon.

She is so good that the rest of the team stops trying to learn. They just send her requests. "Can we see this with a dark background?" "What if the user was a dog?" She becomes a human API for generating images.

She is a hero. The team moves fast.

Then she takes a two-week vacation.

And all visual design work stops cold. The project stalls. Nobody else knows the prompts, the tools, the workflow. Her superpower was a single point of failure for the entire team.

A personal skill is a great start. But a shared playbook is what makes a team resilient. The goal is not to create one wizard who everyone depends on. It's to turn individual magic tricks into a repeatable process the whole team can run.
