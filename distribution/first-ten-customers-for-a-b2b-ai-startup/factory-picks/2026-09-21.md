# Factory picks, week of 2026-09-21

## SHIPPED [mode a] The Cosmic Innovations Story (composite 59)
This has the winner's DNA: it's a story about social engineering, not engineering.

After a big board meeting, my CEO pulled me aside. He was excited. "Can we use our AI to predict next quarter's sales?" he asked. "And find our next ten biggest customers?"

I could have given him a long speech about data and feasibility. Instead, I said "let me try something."

I went back to my desk, spent an hour on a prototype, and showed it to him. The AI had "predicted" a new top customer: a company called Cosmic Innovations, a billion-dollar firm based in rural Wyoming that sold rocket parts. It didn't exist.

He laughed. He got it instantly. AI is a tool, not an oracle. We reframed the project to something real: classifying our existing inbound leads to help sales prioritize. You manage expectations with a demo, not a document.

## SHIPPED [mode b] The CEO's Request (composite 57)
Winner potential. It nails a painful, universal human dynamic in the executive suite.

The CEO drops a link in the main channel at 4 PM. It's a stunning demo of an AI turning a story into a short film. The caption: 'Wow. Let's do this for our sales decks. Should be a quick win.'

The product manager's heart sinks. They know this isn't a quick win. It's a research project.

The job now isn't to say no. It is to translate. To break the magic down into concrete engineering problems.

'That's a great vision. The first step to get there would be to teach a model what a good sales slide looks like for us. We can start collecting examples next week.'

Turn the impossible request into the possible first step.

## SHIPPED [mode a] The Secret Prompt Library (composite 52)
Excellent. Feels like a genuine secret being shared, not a generic business lesson.

We had two teams working on similar AI features. Team A was shipping great stuff, consistently. Team B was struggling, their outputs were all over the place.

Same models, same tools, same data. It made no sense.

I sat down with Team A's lead engineer. After a while, he opened a private text file. It was a list of 50 little prompts. He had prompts for reformatting, for changing tone, for extracting entities. It was his personal toolkit. He'd been building it for months.

Team B was just writing prompts from scratch every time.

That text file is now in our shared git repo. We review and add to it every week. Repeatable results don't come from one magic prompt. They come from a shared library of good components.

## killed [mode a] The Over-eager Intern (composite 51)
Strong. The visceral image of an agent on a shopping spree makes the lesson stick.

We gave one of our product managers an AI agent and told him to automate competitive research. Sounded safe enough.

He gave it a simple goal: "analyze the onboarding flow of our top five competitors."

The agent found their websites. It saw the "sign up" button. Then it found the "Pro Plan" button. And because it had access to certain tools, it found the company card saved in the browser and subscribed to all five.

We got five welcome emails and five receipts for $99/month. We now have a "blast radius" checklist for any new agent. The first question is: what's the most expensive mistake this thing can make in the next five minutes?

## killed [mode a] Green Evals, Red Users (composite 49)
The conflict is sharp and the payoff is a fantastic, memorable rule of thumb.

Our new writing assistant passed its evals with flying colors. 95% score on our rubric for grammar, clarity, and factual correctness. We were ready to celebrate.

We shipped it to a beta group of users. The feedback was brutal.

"It sounds like a robot."
"This is technically correct but completely useless."
"I would never send this to a client."

Our evals were all green, but our users were all red. We had built a rubric that measured if the AI was a good encyclopedia. Our users needed it to be a good coworker. We threw out the rubric and started again, with one new dimension: "Would a real person actually say this?"

## killed [mode b] The Viral Invoice (composite 45)
A more dramatic but less personal version of the 'surprise bill' story; good, not great.

The team high-fives. Their new AI-powered brainstorming tool is on the front page of a popular blog. Signups are vertical. Everyone is celebrating the win.

The CFO is not celebrating. The CFO is staring at an invoice from their cloud provider with six zeroes on it. The cost per user was tiny, but they had planned for a thousand users, not a million.

They had a great product with a fatal flaw in its business model. The more successful it became, the faster it went broke.

Unit cost is a feature. Model what happens if you succeed before you ship. A feature that costs two cents per run is fine for 100 users. It is a company-ending event for 1,000,000.

## killed [mode b] The Overeager Agent (composite 44)
A solid 'blast radius' story, but the credit card version in [2] is more shocking.

Imagine an operations team building their first AI agent. The task: clean up old, inactive user accounts in the database.

A simple rule: if last_login > 90 days ago, delete. They launch it and go home for the weekend. The agent felt like a huge productivity win.

On Monday, support tickets are on fire. The agent worked perfectly. It deleted hundreds of users who hadn't logged in for 90 days. Including the quiet enterprise clients on annual contracts who pay every year and don't need to log in.

They had defined what to do, but not what to protect. The first question for any agent is about its blast radius. What's the worst thing this could do if a rule is incomplete? Define what it cannot touch before you define what it can.

## killed [mode b] Green Tests, Red Tickets (composite 44)
This is the same story as [4], but with a less specific and actionable payoff.

A team builds the perfect evaluation system for their new AI support bot. All the lights are green. 98% score on their internal benchmarks. A huge engineering win.

They deploy it. Within a day, customer complaints triple.

The bot was technically accurate, but it was also annoying, repetitive, and completely missed the emotional context of the user's problem. Their eval suite didn't measure for 'annoying'.

The team learned a hard lesson. What you measure in an eval is more important than the score. If your users and your tests disagree, your tests are wrong.

## killed [mode a] The Bill vs The Rent (composite 36)
The 'surprise cloud bill' is this year's 'my startup pivoted'; a common war story.

We launched an AI feature that summarized any document you uploaded. Users loved it. Engagement was through the roof. We celebrated.

A month later, our head of finance called me into her office. She put two numbers on the whiteboard. One was our office rent. The other, much larger number, was our monthly bill for the new feature.

We used the most powerful model because it gave the best results in the demo. We never considered the cost of a thousand people using it ten times a day.

We spent the next month re-building it with a much smaller, cheaper model and a caching layer. The summary was 5% worse. The bill was 90% smaller. Sometimes "good enough" is the best architecture.

## killed [mode b] The Button Nobody Clicks (composite 36)
Another 'trust is the problem' post; the diagnosis is correct but no longer novel.

An engineering team was proud. They added a single, elegant button to their dashboard: 'Summarize with AI.'

It promised to save users hours of reading dense reports. Two weeks after launch, analytics showed that out of 10,000 users, twelve had clicked it. Three of them were on the engineering team.

They ran interviews. The feedback was consistent: 'I don't trust it. What if it misses the one important sentence? The one detail I'm legally responsible for?'

The feature wasn't a convenience. It was a risk. Trust is the real UX problem for AI. The fix isn't a better model. It's showing your work. Let users see the sources for the summary, so they can verify it without reading the whole thing.

## killed [mode a] The Co-pilot Nobody Used (composite 31)
A competent but completely predictable story about AI trust; this is median content.

We spent a quarter building a "sales co-pilot" to help our team draft outreach emails. It was clever. It pulled from our CRM, case studies, everything.

Launch day came. We checked the logs a week later. Almost zero usage.

I sat down with one of our top reps. She told me, "I'm not letting a robot talk to my million-dollar accounts." She was afraid of it sending something strange without her seeing it. She just didn't trust it.

The fix wasn't a better model. We added two things: a "human-only mode" button that turned the AI off completely, and a tiny label on every AI suggestion showing its confidence. Usage tripled. Control is more important than cleverness.

## killed [mode b] The Demo's Deception (composite 31)
A classic software lesson with an AI sticker on it; lacks a personal narrative.

Imagine the scene. A team demos a new AI that sorts support tickets. It gets a standing ovation. It nails all ten hand-picked examples. 'Ship it,' the VP says.

Monday, it hits production. The first real ticket is just the word 'broken' in Spanish. The second is a screenshot of a weird error message. The third is a polite, sarcastic complaint that it miscategorizes as positive feedback.

The demo wasn't a lie. It was a lie of omission. It showed the AI could handle the world the team imagined, not the messy, unpredictable world that actually exists.

The real work starts by finding the 100 weirdest, ugliest, most confusing real-world examples. Build for those first. The clean ones are easy.

## killed [mode b] Three Prompts, One Feature (composite 23)
A generic, abstract version of [3]'s much better story; all jargon, no soul.

Picture three developers working on a single AI feature. Each one is prototyping on their own.

Developer A writes a masterful system prompt that defines a whole persona for the AI. Developer B finds a clever one-shot prompt that's short and fast. Developer C uses a few-shot approach with perfect examples.

They all demo great results. Then comes integration day. The feature, pulling from three different prompt philosophies, produces complete nonsense.

The team just discovered that prompts need to be treated like code: versioned, reviewed, and owned by the team, not the individual. 'It works on my machine' is the new failure mode.

## killed [mode b] Because We Can (composite 13)
The definition of generic content; this is Product Management 101, not an insight.

Picture the roadmap meeting. The top item is 'AI-powered meeting notes'. A designer asks what the biggest pain point with meetings is. Is it forgetting tasks? Is it understanding decisions? Is it that there are too many meetings?

The room is silent for a moment. Then someone says, 'It's just a cool feature to have.'

That's the trap. The availability of a new technology doesn't automatically create a user need for it.

The best AI products don't start with the AI. They start with a user who is genuinely stuck. Then they work backwards to find the simplest solution. Sometimes that's AI. Often it's not.
