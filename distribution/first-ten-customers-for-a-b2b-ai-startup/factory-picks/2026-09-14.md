# Factory picks, week of 2026-09-14

## SHIPPED [mode a] The F1 Car Commute (composite 65)
This is an almost perfect execution of the formula; a powerful, visceral analogy with a sharp business payoff.

My friend built a race car in his garage. An engineering marvel. 0 to 60 in under three seconds.

He took it to get groceries once.

It cost him ninety dollars in fuel, deafened the entire street, and he couldn't even fit the milk in the passenger seat.

That's your AI demo. A stunning piece of performance on one perfect lap. But it is not ready for the daily commute of production traffic.

Before you ship, stop asking about its top speed. Ask what it costs to run ten thousand trips to the store.

## SHIPPED [mode a] The Overeager Apprentice (composite 62)
A perfectly targeted analogy for the current moment in AI development with a critical, actionable payoff.

We asked a junior engineer to fix a small bug on the login page. A simple ticket. Should have taken an hour.

We came back the next day and he had completely re-written our user database schema. He was trying to be 'proactive,' but he broke the entire application.

He didn't have bad intentions. He just didn't know the boundaries.

We are pointing AI coding agents at our repositories with the same lack of scope. We give them a vague goal and admin access.

Give your AI a specific file and a narrow goal. Never say 'go optimize the checkout flow.' Leash your apprentice.

## killed [mode a] The Intern's Phone Bill (composite 59)
A fantastic, memorable story with a crucial payoff; a slightly slow hook holds it back from perfection.

Years ago, we hired a sales intern. We gave him a phone, a list of leads, and told him to 'be persistent.'

We forgot to give him a budget. Or a stop signal.

He spent two full days calling a single lead in rural Australia because they were 'hard to reach.' The phone bill was larger than his first paycheck.

We're now deploying AI agents with the same vague instructions and access to expensive tools.

The most important instruction you can give an agent isn't what to do. It's when to quit.

## killed [mode a] This Soda Or That One (composite 56)
A clean, powerful analogy with a very practical takeaway for a sophisticated audience.

My first marketing gig, we ran a focus group for a new soda. We asked people to rate it on a scale of 1 to 10.

The data was a mess. Sevens and eights and threes. Totally useless for making a decision.

The next time, we put our soda next to the market leader. We just asked, 'Which one do you prefer?'

The signal was instant and clear.

Stop asking your LLM to rate its own output on some imaginary scale. It's just guessing. Give it two versions and ask it to pick the better one. It's the fastest way to find out what 'good' actually means.

## killed [mode a] One Good Example (composite 54)
Strong execution of the winning formula, but the core idea is becoming a bit too familiar.

We hired a new analyst and gave him a 30-page guide on how to build the weekly sales report. Definitions, data sources, formatting rules. Everything.

He spent three days on it. It was still wrong.

With the next analyst, we tried something else. We gave her last week's finished report and said, 'Make it look like this, with the new numbers.'

She was done in two hours.

We do this with our AI all the time. We write encyclopedias of instructions and system prompts, trying to explain a task. When we should just show it one good example of what 'done' looks like.

## killed [mode a] The Psychic Vending Machine (composite 53)
A great, memorable story, but the payoff feels a little less sharp than the others.

Our old office installed a 'smart' vending machine. It had a camera that tried to predict what you wanted based on your facial expression.

It was wrong almost every time. It offered people peanuts when they wanted water.

After week one, nobody used it. We just wanted to press B4 and get our chips. We wanted a tool, not a mind-reader.

Your AI feature might be too clever. Users often don't want magic they can't control or predict. They want a reliable way to get the job done.

Sometimes the best user experience is just a simple button.

## killed [mode a] The Secret Ingredient (composite 51)
A solid, well-told analogy for a real problem, but lacks the sharp, surprising edge of the top contenders.

I once worked in a restaurant where two different cooks made the house sauce. The recipe was on the wall.

But one used three cloves of garlic, the other used four. One swore by a different brand of vinegar. Each had their 'personal method.'

Customers constantly complained the sauce tasted different every night.

This is your team using AI. Everyone has their own 'magic prompt' for the same customer support task. The results are chaotic and inconsistent.

Turn personal tricks into a team playbook. Find the best prompt, version it, and make it the standard. Consistency is a feature.

## killed [mode a] The Sad Whale Report (composite 39)
A good anecdote with a great payoff, but the weak hook and non-existent reveal kill its outlier potential.

Our new transcription AI was flawless in testing. Perfect summaries, accurate speaker labels, the works.

First week in production, it transcribed our CEO's mid-sentence sigh as 'a small, sad whale.'

There was a moment of panic. Then we started the 'weird log.' Every strange, funny, or baffling output went in there. A ghost in the machine list.

That log is now our most valuable asset. It's the regression test suite we could never have designed. It catches failures that formal evals miss.

Don't just fix the weird outputs. Collect them. Your production surprises are your best teachers.

## SHIPPED [mode b] Computation Or Negotiation (composite 39)
A truly brilliant insight and a strong hook, but the case-study format without a reveal holds it back.

A CEO sees a demo online and tells the team to build an AI that writes the product roadmap. The instruction is "just feed it our backlog and customer interviews."

The team spends a quarter building it. It looks great. It ingests thousands of data points and produces a perfectly formatted, plausible-looking roadmap.

They launch it internally. Nobody on the product team uses it more than once.

They realized that building a roadmap isn't a summarization problem. It's a negotiation problem. It's about arguments, trade-offs, and getting smart people to agree on a direction. The AI could generate a list, but it couldn't create alignment.

Before you build, ask: is the core task computation, or is it human negotiation? AI is only good at the first one.

## killed [mode b] The Four-Point Rubric (composite 38)
A crucial piece of practical advice, but the direct format makes it feel like a standard blog post.

Imagine a team is building an AI to generate project plans. For weeks, every review meeting is a painful debate.

One PM says an output "feels too generic." An engineer argues it "followed all the instructions." The director says it "lacks strategic alignment."

It's all vibes. They make zero progress.

Finally, the lead PM walks to the whiteboard and writes four questions:
1. Does it list concrete tasks?
2. Does every task have an owner?
3. Is the timeline under 4 weeks?
4. Can a VP understand it in 30 seconds?

That's it. That's the rubric. The arguments stop. They start evaluating outputs against the list, and within a week, the quality is 10x better.

Stop debating if AI output is 'good'. Write a simple checklist for what 'good' means. A rubric turns opinions into measurements.

## killed [mode a] The AI-Powered Toaster (composite 37)
Great hook, but it's an example, not an analogy, which kills the reveal and makes the payoff feel generic.

A friend of mine worked at a smart-home startup that spent six months building an AI-powered toaster.

It could analyze bread density and ambient humidity to calculate the perfect toast. It had a companion app. It cost $400.

They sold almost none.

You know what people want from a toaster? Toast. Reasonably fast and not burnt.

This is happening everywhere with AI right now. We're adding brilliant, expensive complexity to problems that don't need it. We're building AI toasters.

Before asking 'can we build this with AI,' ask 'who wants this and what job does it actually do better?'

## killed [mode b] The Right-Sized Model (composite 37)
The $20k bill is a great hook, but the story is a straightforward case study for a common piece of advice.

A team prototypes a new feature using the top-of-the-line model. It's a simple categorization tool for incoming feedback. The demo is impressive and it works flawlessly.

They ship it.

The first monthly bill is $20,000.

The VP of Engineering almost kills the project. But one engineer runs a test. She swaps out the massive model for a smaller, open-source one that costs pennies per call. She tests 1,000 outputs.

The cheaper model is 98% as good. For a task that just adds internal tags, 98% is perfect.

Nobody using the product notices the change. The finance department definitely notices the new bill.

Don't use the biggest model by default. Use the cheapest one that is good enough.

## killed [mode b] Deleting The Prompt (composite 30)
A useful tip presented as a bland story that feels like every other 'how to prompt' article.

A product manager spends two weeks crafting the perfect system prompt. It's ten pages long. It details the brand's tone of voice, its target audience, and has a dozen rules for generating marketing copy.

He gives it to the engineering team. The AI's output is bland, corporate, and follows almost none of the rules.

They spend another week tweaking phrases. Nothing works.

Finally, a junior developer gets frustrated. He deletes the entire ten-page document. He replaces it with just three things: three examples of their best-performing ads from last quarter, with no other instructions.

The next output is perfect. Better than their human copywriters.

Stop trying to explain the rules. Just show the AI your best work.

## killed [mode b] The Blast Radius Question (composite 30)
An absolutely critical lesson, but the storytelling is too dry to make it an outlier.

Consider a team building an AI agent to clean up their cloud resources. It's supposed to find and delete unused servers to save money.

They give it broad permissions to their entire cloud account. For a month, it's a hero, saving them thousands of dollars.

Then one Monday morning, nobody can log in. The agent found a database critical for production, saw it had a tag from a test two years ago, and deleted it. The tag was old, but the agent was just following its rules.

The outage lasts 12 hours.

Before you give an agent the power to act, ask one question: what's the absolute worst thing it can do on a bad day? Then shrink its permissions until the answer is boring.

## killed [mode b] Prompts are Infrastructure (composite 29)
A practical solution to a real problem, presented as a boring story that reads like a textbook.

Picture a company where sales, marketing, and product all use AI for summarizing reports. It's a mess.

Sales has a prompt in a shared Google Doc. Marketing's best prompt is in an old Slack message nobody can find. The product team's prompt is a text file on one PM's laptop, and he's on vacation.

When a new model is released, all the prompts start failing in subtle ways. Nobody knows what the official prompt even is, so nobody can fix it.

They solve it by treating prompts like code. They create a folder in GitHub. Every prompt gets a clear owner, a version number, and any change requires a review from one other person.

Personal AI tricks are a liability. Repeatable results come from treating prompts like the infrastructure they are.

## killed [mode b] The Weirdness Log (composite 27)
A valuable lesson buried in a generic case study format that lacks any narrative punch.

A team ships an AI summary tool for customer calls. The demo was perfect.

Week one in production, it summarizes a 30-minute complaint about a faulty server into a single sentence: "The customer is interested in our cloud hosting options."

It's technically not wrong. The customer did mention the cloud. But it's useless.

The first instinct is to patch the prompt. Add a rule about negative sentiment. But one engineer does something different. She starts a document called the 'Weird Log' and adds this as the first entry.

Two months later, the log has 50 entries. It's a goldmine of edge cases and subtle user needs. It becomes their evaluation suite, a better guide than any prompt instruction they could have written.

Your first production outputs are not bugs to be fixed. They are requirements to be understood.
