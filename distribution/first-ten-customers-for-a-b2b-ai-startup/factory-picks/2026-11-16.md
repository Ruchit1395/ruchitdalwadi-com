# Factory picks, week of 2026-11-16

## SHIPPED [mode b] The First 50 Runs (composite 63)
This is it. 'The bug was a feeling' is the kind of line that changes how people think.

A team ships their first AI feature. It summarizes customer calls. They set up alerts for server errors and crashes.

For two weeks, the dashboard is all green. But support tickets are climbing.

A PM digs in. The summaries are technically perfect. But one summarized a call where a customer mentioned their daughter's graduation. The AI suggested a follow-up email to "congratulate them on the successful graduation event."

It was correct, but it felt creepy.

The bug wasn't a 500 error. The bug was a feeling.

Your first 50 production runs aren't for catching crashes. They're for catching weirdness. Don't just log the output. Log the human reaction: Did they edit it? Copy it? Delete it immediately? That's your real test suite.

## SHIPPED [mode b] One Good Example (composite 63)
This uses the winner's formula: a perfect, non-obvious analogy that reframes everything.

Picture onboarding a new junior designer.

You could give them a 40-page brand manifesto full of rules. "Our tone is authoritative yet approachable." "Colors must evoke trust."

Or you could send them a link to the last three landing pages the team shipped and say, "make the new one feel like these."

Which one produces better work on day one?

Your LLM is that new junior designer. It learns from examples, not from abstract rules. We see teams waste weeks adding more adjectives to their system prompt, trying to describe the perfect output.

Stop describing. Show it. One high-quality example of the output you want is worth a thousand words of instruction.

## killed [mode b] When Users Beat Evals (composite 62)
Excellent. It exposes the fatal flaw in metric-driven AI development. Very sharp.

A team built an incredible evaluation suite for their AI writing assistant. It measured grammar, clarity, brevity, and factual accuracy. They had a single score that told them if a new model was better or worse.

A new update scored a 99.2%, the highest ever. They shipped it with confidence.

Within a day, users were complaining. They hated it.

The team was confused. The outputs were technically perfect. So they talked to the users. A sales rep explained: "My emails don't sound like me anymore. They're too clean. Too perfect."

The model, in its pursuit of brevity and clarity, had stripped out all the personality.

When your eval suite disagrees with your users, the suite is wrong. Always. The goal is not a higher score, it's a happier user.

## SHIPPED [mode a] The Expert's Magic (composite 56)
Strong. It correctly identifies that scaling genius is the real problem.

Our lead designer started using an AI image tool for mockups. His output doubled overnight, and the quality was stunning. We were blown away.

Management decided everyone on the design team should use it. 'Let's scale his process,' they said.

We bought 10 licenses. It was a disaster.

Nobody could replicate his results. They didn't know his specific prompts, his chain of tools, his editing techniques. His personal genius was not a documented workflow. It was a thousand tiny habits he couldn't even explain.

We learned a lesson. An expert's personal magic is not a team process. A team process is something you can write down on a single page and hand to a brand new hire.

## killed [mode b] The Adoption Label (composite 55)
The 'critic vs. collaborator' payoff is brilliant. A masterclass in UX psychology.

A product team builds an AI that drafts sales emails. To be transparent, they add a label at the top of each draft: "Generated by AI."

They ship it to the sales team. Usage is almost zero.

They interview the reps. The feedback is consistent: "I'm not sending an email written by a robot. That's not my voice."

The team changes just two things. They change the label to "Suggested draft." And they make the 'edit' button more prominent.

Adoption triples overnight.

The feature didn't change, but the frame did. "Generated by AI" makes the user a critic. "Suggested draft" makes them a collaborator. It's their draft now, just with a head start.

## killed [mode b] The Weekend Bill (composite 51)
A visceral horror story. The five-figure bill makes the lesson stick.

Imagine a team builds a chatbot for internal company questions. It's hooked up to all their internal docs. A developer, trying to be thorough, points an automated monitoring script at it.

The script pings the bot's health check endpoint every 60 seconds. It just asks, "Are you okay?"

The helpful chatbot interprets this as a real question. It replies with a detailed, multi-thousand word summary of the company's entire knowledge base.

Every minute. All weekend.

On Monday, the cloud bill has an extra five figures on it. The cost wasn't from a million users. It was from one script, a vague prompt, and no budget alert.

Cost controls aren't an optimization task. They are a day one feature. Set a hard budget and a kill switch before the first API call.

## killed [mode b] The AI Login Quote (composite 51)
A necessary critique of 'AI theater' that every exec needs to read.

A product meeting is stuck. The CEO wants to know what their AI strategy is. Someone has an idea: "What if we used AI to generate a unique, inspirational quote for every user when they log in?"

Everyone loves it. It's visible. It uses AI. It's an easy win.

The team spends a month building it. It works. The quotes are surprisingly good. They ship it.

And nothing happens.

Users log in to do a job. They are not waiting to be inspired. They click past the quote to get to their work. The feature is a success and a total waste of time.

Using AI is not an outcome. Solving a user's problem is the outcome. If the AI feature doesn't solve one of their top three problems, it's a distraction.

## killed [mode a] The Silent Break (composite 50)
The 'it broke silently' reveal is powerful. A real operational insight.

The first AI we shipped categorized incoming support tickets. It was a huge success. The support team loved it.

Three months later, the support manager told me it was broken. Everything was being mislabeled as 'Other.'

We panicked. We checked the logs. No errors. We checked the code. No new deploys. We checked the model provider's status page. Nothing.

The tool hadn't changed at all. The problem was our customers.

We had just launched a major new product. Customers were writing in using a whole new vocabulary we'd never trained the model on. The world had changed, and the AI was still living three months in the past. It broke silently because the data drifted.

## killed [mode b] The Blast Radius Question (composite 44)
A good cautionary tale, but 'don't give robots your wallet' isn't a shocking reveal.

A team builds an agent to help with customer support. They give it a tool that can read tickets. They give it another tool that can write draft responses. The demo goes great.

For the next demo, they connect a new tool: the ability to issue refunds via Stripe. An agent's job is to solve problems, right?

They give it a test ticket: a user complaining about a $10 billing error.

The agent misunderstands. It finds the customer's largest-ever invoice for $10,000 and helpfully refunds the entire amount.

Before you give an agent a new tool, ask one question: what is the absolute worst thing this can do if it misunderstands the goal? If the answer involves sending money, deleting users, or deploying code, put a human approval step in front of it. Period.

## killed [mode a] The Five Minute Form (composite 43)
A decent anecdote, but the punchline is a chuckle, not a revelation.

One of our most clever engineers spent a month building an AI agent to automate his expense reports. He demoed it to the team. You'd forward it receipts, and it would fill out the reimbursement form automatically.

It was a cool piece of technology. It worked about 80% of the time.

Then somebody asked how long it took him to do expenses before. He said about five minutes a week.

And how long does it take now? About ten minutes, because he has to check the agent's work and fix the errors.

We praised his ingenuity and technical skill. Then, a few weeks later, we quietly went back to filling out the five-minute form by hand.

## killed [mode a] The Pointless Argument (composite 24)
A story about a normal Tuesday meeting with 'AI' sprinkled on top.

Two product managers were in a heated debate over two versions of an AI feature. The first version's output was, in one PM's words, 'more creative and interesting.' The second version, according to the other PM, was 'more accurate and on-brand.'

They were both right. The argument was going nowhere.

They had been arguing for 30 minutes about which one was 'better' without ever defining what 'better' meant. We stopped the meeting.

We took out a whiteboard and wrote down four questions. Is it accurate? Is it helpful? Does it match our voice? Is it concise? We agreed to score each output on those four dimensions from 1 to 5.

Suddenly, the argument was over. We had a rubric. We weren't debating feelings anymore. We were measuring against a spec.

## killed [mode a] The Giant Approve Button (composite 19)
This is the median AI post. Competent, correct, and completely forgettable.

We built an AI tool for our sales team to draft follow-up emails. We used the best model and engineered the perfect prompt. We showed them how it saved 30 minutes a day.

Nobody used it.

We asked for feedback. We assumed the email quality wasn't good enough. That wasn't it at all. The emails were fine.

The problem was trust. They were afraid it would invent a fake discount or get a client's name wrong. They couldn't risk it.

The fix had nothing to do with the model. We added a giant 'Approve' button. The AI would draft the email, and they had to click the button to send it. They could see exactly what was going out.

Adoption went up 80% the next week. The problem was never the AI. It was the user interface.

## killed [mode a] The 400-Page Report (composite 16)
A blog post about a technical mistake, not an insight about people.

We built an agent to research competitors. We gave it a simple task: 'Find three new startups in the marketing automation space.'

It started running. An hour passed. Then two. It was still running that evening.

It finally finished after six hours. The agent had spun up multiple cloud instances and produced a 400-page PDF on the entire history of marketing automation, dating back to the 1990s. It was impressive. It was also completely useless.

We gave it a goal, but we never told it when to stop. We never defined what 'done' looked like.

Now, every agent project at our company starts with the same question: what is the single, specific output that tells this thing its job is over?
