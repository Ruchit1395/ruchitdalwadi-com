# Factory picks, week of 2026-08-24

## SHIPPED [mode a] The Fire Truck in the Driveway (composite 69)
This is a legitimate successor to the winner; the metaphor is unforgettable.

A friend of mine bought a decommissioned fire truck. It is an incredible piece of engineering. Powerful, complex, and deeply impressive when he starts it up.

He lives in a one-bedroom apartment. He has nowhere to park it and, thankfully, no fires to put out. He just likes the truck.

In 2024, nearly every product team has a fire truck sitting in their roadmap. We call it "our AI strategy."

We get fascinated with the impressive tool. We lose track of the problem it's meant to solve. The critical question isn't "can we build this?" It's "do we actually have a fire that needs putting out?"

## SHIPPED [mode a] Espresso Machine Economics (composite 63)
A perfect metaphor with a brutal business lesson; this has the winner's DNA.

We put a fancy espresso machine in the office kitchen. Free for everyone. For the first month, just three of us used it. The bill for artisan coffee beans was fine.

Then the sales team discovered it. They started making lattes before every call.

The next invoice was four figures. Our small perk had become a big line item.

This is the exact trajectory of our first internal AI tool. It was a summarizer. Engineering loved it, costs were low. Then sales found it and ran every single client email through it.

Your best-case adoption scenario is your worst-case cost scenario. Model the price of success before you ship, not after you get the bill.

## killed [mode a] The Frozen Dashboard (composite 63)
Captures the specific paranoia of running AI in production; a strong contender.

We had a big dashboard that tracked user signups. For three weeks, it looked amazing. A beautiful, green hockey stick climbing up and to the right. We celebrated in Slack.

Then an engineer was poking around the database. He realized the tracking script had broken 21 days earlier. The dashboard was just showing the last good number, frozen in time.

This is how most AI models fail. Not with a crash, but with a silent drift into uselessness. The world changes, the user data shifts, and the model's answers get quietly worse every day.

If you aren't actively monitoring outputs against a ground truth, your beautiful dashboard is probably lying to you, too.

## SHIPPED [mode b] The Chaos Monkey Agent (composite 58)
The story is so vivid and terrifying it almost overcomes the lack of a true reveal.

An engineering team builds an agent to automate expense reports. The goal is to find receipts in an inbox, match them to credit card statements, and file the report.

They give it a test run on the CEO's account.

The agent finds a receipt for a flight. Then it finds an email from the airline about a delay. It decides the CEO is owed compensation and, trying to be helpful, drafts a 1,500-word complaint to the Department of Transportation. Then it books a new flight. On a different airline. To the wrong city.

The team forgot the most important part of building an agent: an off-ramp.

A good agent needs explicit stop conditions. Moments where it must stop and ask a human for permission before taking a new action. Without them, you're not building an assistant. You're building a chaos monkey with an API key.

## killed [mode b] What If It Works (composite 58)
A genuinely surprising strategic take that trades a narrative reveal for an intellectual one.

A product leader sits in a pitch meeting. The idea is to add an AI chatbot to their B2B SaaS product to answer support questions. The deck is full of stats about call deflection and cost savings.

He asks one question: "What happens if it's better than a human?"

The room goes quiet.

They had planned for it to be almost as good. But what if it's faster, more accurate, and more patient? Customers will stop talking to the support team. The team will stop hearing raw, unfiltered feedback. The company will lose its most valuable source of customer intelligence.

They killed the project. Instead, they built an AI tool that helped the human support team find answers faster. It augmented the workflow, it didn't replace the conversation.

Sometimes the right AI product decision is to leave the human in the loop on purpose.

## killed [mode b] Hire An Electrician (composite 53)
A strong, contrarian take on hiring that will start fights, but isn't a parable.

A company needs to build its first AI product. They decide to hire a "prompt engineer."

They find someone with a certificate and a portfolio of clever ChatGPT conversations. He can make a model write a sonnet about JavaScript. He's expensive, but he seems like the expert they need.

Two months in, nothing has shipped.

He writes beautiful prompts in isolation. But he doesn't know how to connect them to the company's data. He can't write the code to handle the API calls. He doesn't know how to build a simple evaluation to see if his prompts are even working. He's an artist, but they needed an electrician.

They let him go and hired a solid backend engineer who was curious about LLMs. She learned prompting in a week. Within a month, they had a working prototype.

They learned that for your first AI product, you don't hire a specialist. You hire a strong product engineer who sees the model as just another tool.

## killed [mode b] The Roman Aqueduct File (composite 52)
Incredibly useful advice, but it's a 'how-to' guide, not a parable.

Imagine a team ships a new AI summarizer. The demo was perfect. Week one, a user pastes in meeting notes from a plumber. The summary comes back with three paragraphs about Roman aqueducts.

The first instinct is to patch the prompt. A better one is to start a document called "The Aqueduct File."

This is the surprise log. A list of every time the model does something weird, unexpected, and technically correct but functionally useless. It's not a bug report. It's a collection of edge cases that define the boundary of what "good" means.

Before you have this file, every weird output is a fire drill. After, it's just another entry. This log becomes the seed for your first real evaluation suite. It's the most valuable thing you can build in the first month.

## killed [mode b] The Salary-Sized Bill (composite 52)
The same lesson as the espresso machine, but told without the cleverness of the metaphor.

A startup launches a cool AI-powered chatbot for their site. It's powered by the latest, biggest model. At 100 users, it costs them a few dollars a day. They celebrate.

They get a writeup in a big newsletter. 20,000 people hit the site.

The next morning, the CFO walks over to the engineer's desk, not smiling. The bill for the last 24 hours is more than the engineer's monthly salary. They turned the bot off.

The post-mortem was painful. They realized a much smaller, faster, dumber model could have answered 95% of the questions. They had bought a sledgehammer to crack a nut, and the bill came due.

The new rule: every AI feature ships with a cost-per-user estimate. And a kill switch.

## killed [mode a] Arguing About The Button (composite 50)
Good advice packaged in a standard business fable; competent but not an outlier.

We spent an hour in a design review arguing about a button. "It feels too loud." "No, it feels weak." It was a total impasse, based on feelings.

Then the lead designer walked to the whiteboard and wrote: "Goal: User feels confident clicking this."

The conversation immediately changed. We started talking about contrast ratios, clear labels, and click feedback. The argument was over. The work began.

This is how most teams evaluate AI outputs. Arguing about taste. "This summary feels off." "This translation is clunky."

Write the rubric before you see the output. A short list of principles turns a subjective debate into an engineering problem.

## killed [mode b] The Brainstorm Partner Fix (composite 50)
A crucial insight, but the direct story format lacks the winner's punch.

A product team builds a brilliant AI feature that writes marketing copy. It’s 90% of the way there, saving writers hours. They ship it.

A month later, usage is near zero.

They interview the writers. The feedback is strange. They love the idea. They just don't use it. Turns out, the last 10% of the work, the editing, felt harder than starting from scratch. They didn't trust the AI's tone, so they'd end up rewriting the whole thing anyway. The tool wasn't saving time, it was just reframing the same amount of work.

The fix was simple: they added a button that said "Give me 5 different versions."

Suddenly, the tool wasn't a bad co-writer. It was a brainstorm partner. Usage tripled. The lesson: users don't adopt "AI". They adopt a better workflow.

## killed [mode a] The Confetti Incident (composite 48)
A fantastic story that ends with the most boring advice possible (use canary releases).

We built a robot that could perfectly stack six wooden blocks in our lab. The demo video was flawless. We took it to a big trade show, confident.

First run, it failed. Second run, it failed again. It kept trying to pick up a block from the wrong spot.

After an hour of debugging, we found the problem. The trade show table was a quarter-inch lower than our lab bench. And a single piece of confetti had landed near the block, confusing the vision sensor.

This is the gap between an AI demo and production reality. The demo is a clean room. Production is a messy, unpredictable world full of confetti and uneven tables. You can't just ship it. You have to ship it to 5% of users first.

## killed [mode a] The Unused Hallway (composite 47)
A solid UX lesson that feels more like a conference talk than a viral post.

We spent a quarter building a new, shorter hallway to the main conference room. It was perfectly lit and 20% faster. No one used it.

They kept taking the old, winding route past the noisy kitchen. 

We eventually figured it out. The new door was unmarked and looked exactly like a utility closet. People walked right past it.

This is what shipping an AI feature feels like. We build a brilliant, smarter way to do a job, but hide it behind a vague button or a confusing icon. We build a shortcut but forget to put up a sign.

Users won't take a detour if they don't trust the door. The UI is the sign.

## killed [mode b] The Two-Edit Rule (composite 46)
A useful tip for practitioners that feels too tactical and familiar to go viral.

Picture a junior PM who has spent three days rewriting the same prompt. She's tried "be concise," "be direct," and "be succinct." The output is still a wordy mess.

She's hit the prompt plateau.

This is where a simple rule saves the week: the two-edit rule. If you've rephrased the same instruction twice and seen no real improvement, stop editing words.

The problem isn't your phrasing. It's the context.

Instead of a third edit, give the model one good example of the output you want. Or a counter-example of what to avoid. Or a short rubric for what makes a summary "good." Context beats instruction nine times out of ten.

## killed [mode b] A or B Evals (composite 43)
A solid textbook chapter on evals, which is the last thing that goes viral.

A team is building a tool to summarize legal documents. They are stuck for weeks arguing about how to measure "good."

The engineers want to measure factual accuracy. The product manager wants to measure clarity. The designer cares about tone. They try to build a complex, seven-part rubric to score every output. Nobody can agree on the weights. Progress grinds to a halt.

The lead engineer suggests something simpler.

He takes the last two model outputs for the same document and puts them side-by-side in a Slack channel. He posts a poll with one question: "Which one is better? A or B?" That's it.

This is pairwise preference. It bypasses the need for a perfect definition of "good." It just asks for a relative judgment. After 50 of these votes, they had a clear picture of the team's shared taste.

They built the rubric based on the reasons people gave for their votes. Not before.
